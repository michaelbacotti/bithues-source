There is a small, stubborn fact about the way Americans read in late July. The books that show up in our hands during the second half of the month are rarely the books we planned for. The novel on the nightstand in the first week of July is usually a summer project, something chosen with intention, with a beach in mind, with a pool float and a tote. The novel on the nightstand in the last week of July is more often something that found us, that arrived through a friend or a bookstore clerk or a long enough string of mentions that we stopped resisting and picked it up. *Everything Was Beautiful and Nothing Hurt*, the debut novel by Ben Reeves published on July 7 by Avid Reader Press, is the kind of book that finds people this way. It is now the number one Indie Next Pick for July, it has been on the New York Times bestseller list for two weeks, and the readers who have already finished it are describing it in a tone that is unusual for mid-summer. They are not saying it is a page turner. They are saying it changed something small in the way they think about an ordinary Tuesday.

This is a meditation on that book, and on why mid-July is when a book like this one has its real opening.

## What the Book Understands About Presence

The premise is simple and very old. A man named Travis is Death, working in the modern world. He wears jeans. He lives in a small, grey town. He arrives at the end of a life and offers comfort to the person whose life it has been. He is stoic, gentle, and a little naive despite everything he knows. This is a setup that could become either saccharine or bleak, and Reeves, to his considerable credit, keeps it from becoming either. What he does instead is something stranger and more useful. He makes Travis ordinary. Death, in this novel, is a working person. He has routines. He has a favorite diner. He has a small, quiet friendship with a woman at the laundromat who does not know what he does. He reads paperback novels on his breaks. He is, in every way that matters, the kind of person you would sit next to on a long bus ride and not quite remember the next day, except that he is also the last person you will ever see.

The novel understands something about presence that most fiction about dying does not. Most novels that approach death treat it as an event, the way we treat it in real life: as a thing that happens to other people, eventually, somewhere far away, on a day we will plan for later. Reeves treats it as a practice. Travis is good at his work in the same way a hospice nurse is good at his work. He has learned to be in a room without taking the room over. He has learned to listen without fixing. He has learned to be present, fully and without commentary, at the exact hour when presence is the only thing that matters. There is no drama in this. There is also, it turns out, no lesson more important.

The first thing the book knows is that death is mostly hours. It is the small Tuesday before the appointment. It is the morning the day after the funeral. It is the long stretch of years after, when the people who remain are still figuring out how to live in rooms the deceased no longer occupies. Fiction usually skips these hours. Reeves sits inside them, patiently, with the same quiet attention Travis brings to his work, and the novel becomes, almost without intending to, a manual for how to stay present in the company of someone who is leaving.

## What the Book Understands About Grief

There is a moment, about a third of the way through the novel, when Reeves does something that should not work and absolutely does. He lets the reader see the life on the other side of a death Travis has just attended. Not the death itself. The life. The specific, ordinary life of the person who died, the small rituals they had, the people who loved them, the way the day they died will look, from the inside, to the people who wake up the next morning and find out. The novel does not do this often, and it does not linger. It moves on. But the moment lands with a weight that is bigger than the page it occupies, because the reader has been, for a few hundred pages, looking at life from the wrong end. They have been inside the witness. Reeves briefly turns them around and lets them see what the witness was witnessing, and it is almost too much, in the way only true things are too much.

This is the second thing the book understands about grief, and it is the more important one. Grief is not about the person who died. Grief is about the world that continues, and the strange, accusatory way that world keeps being ordinary. Reeves does not sentimentalize this. He does not turn the survivors into symbols. He treats them as people, the way Travis treats the dying as people, and the effect is to remind the reader that grief is, at its core, an excess of ordinary. There are too many meals to cook. There is too much mail. The clock keeps ticking in the kitchen in the way it has always ticked, and the tapping is, for a while, unbearable, and then it is not, and then it is unbearable again. The novel knows this rhythm because the novel trusts its reader to know it too.

## Who This Book Is For

This is the kind of book that is best read in the way one reads during the slow middle of summer, when the calendar has not yet begun to demand anything in particular. It is for readers who have recently lost someone and are not ready for a memoir about it. It is for readers who have not recently lost anyone and want to be reminded, gently, of what an ordinary life actually means. It is for readers who have been spending the season with the noisy novels, the loud thrillers, the books built to outrun a beach afternoon, and who are ready, on a Tuesday evening with nothing on the calendar, for a book that asks nothing of them except to stay.

It is also, and this is worth saying, for readers who are afraid of books about death. Reeves has written a book that is not afraid of death. It is a book that has, instead, a strange and useful tenderness toward it, and the tenderness is not morbid, and the book is not morbid, and the experience of reading it is closer to sitting beside a hospice window on an afternoon when the light is good than it is to anything that happens in a cemetery.

## A Note on Format

I read *Everything Was Beautiful and Nothing Hurt* in print, and that is the format I would suggest. The prose has a quiet cadence that rewards the eye, and there are passages, especially in the second half, that benefit from being read twice, and a printed page tolerates a second reading in a way that a screen does not. The chapters are short. The book can be read in three or four sittings or in one long afternoon. The audiobook exists and is well-reviewed, and the voice actor who plays Travis has apparently found a register that is exactly right. Audio is a good option for readers who want to be read to on a long drive, and it is the right format for readers who want the experience of someone else holding the room while the story moves. ## A Closing

There is a particular kind of book that finds us in mid-July. It is rarely the book we planned for, and it is almost always the book we needed. *Everything Was Beautiful and Nothing Hurt* is that kind of book. It arrived without urgency. It is asking nothing of the reader except the patience to stay in the room with Travis while he does his quiet work. It is the right book for a week like this one, when nothing in particular is being asked of anyone, and when a reader has a few hours to sit with something that will leave a small, accurate weight on the rest of the summer.

The point is not that death is good, or that grief is sweet, or that the ordinary hours are sacred. The point is that someone thought about those hours carefully enough to write them down, and that the writer trusted the reader enough to leave the hours, mostly, uncommented. That is the thing worth taking into the rest of the week. Not a verdict on the book. A practice: notice one ordinary hour, on purpose, this week, the way Travis would notice it, and stay with it longer than feels necessary.
