He had not opened the tote bag since Tuesday. The tote bag was the tote bag of summer, which was the tote bag he had carried to the farmers' market on Saturdays and to the hardware store on Sundays and to a friend's wedding on the third Saturday of June, where the tote bag had been the bag in which he had carried, on the train home, a paperback a friend at the wedding had pressed into his hand at the reception.

The friend had said, Read this when the air changes. She had not been specific about when the air was going to change. He had taken the paperback home on the train and read the first chapter and a half and then put it in the tote bag with the rest of the things tote bags accumulate, which is to say a paper bag of cherries, a folded canvas shopping bag, two pens and a pencil that had lost its eraser, a tube of sunscreen, and a paperback he had been reading for book club.

The book club paperback had been finished in July. The cherries had been eaten. The sunscreen had been used and had stained the canvas in a long S shape. The paperback from the wedding had been at the bottom the whole time. He had not been looking for it. He had been looking, on the fourth Sunday of August, for the sunglasses he had been missing since Wednesday.

The fourth Sunday of August was the morning the tote bag decided to give the paperback back.

He emptied the tote bag on the kitchen counter on the fourth Sunday of August. The peaches were from yesterday's market. The folded canvas shopping bag had been washed. The two pens and the pencil were the pens he had been looking for since Tuesday. The paperback was at the bottom, with a peach stain on the cover and a crease at page twenty-eight, and a corner of page twenty-eight had been folded, by someone who was not him, into a small triangle the size of a thumbnail.

The triangle had been folded by the friend who had pressed the paperback into his hand at the wedding. The friend was a reader who folded corners instead of using bookmarks. The friend had been, in the manner of friends who fold corners, specific about which page the corner was going to mark. The page was page twenty-eight, which was the second chapter, which was the chapter the paperback had been waiting, at the bottom of a tote bag since the third Saturday of June, for someone to read.

He read the second chapter on the porch. The porch was the porch of August, which has its own opinions about how a man should hold a paperback he has not read in ten weeks. The coffee was on the table. The sunglasses had been on top of his head. The fourth Sunday of August was, by the peach stain and the page twenty-eight crease, the morning the porch had decided to ask him to read.

The second chapter was about a man who returns to a town he had not been to since his mother died. The man in the chapter was not the man on the porch. The man in the chapter was the kind of man a reader meets in order to find out whether the book is the kind of book the reader is going to read. The chapter was not long. The chapter was the kind of chapter a reader reads on a porch on a Sunday in late summer when the porch has decided the morning is going to be a slow morning.

He read the third chapter. The third chapter was about the man and the man's sister driving to the town. The sister was a quiet woman with a careful way of parking. The third chapter was, by the third chapter, the chapter a reader reads in order to find out whether the man and the man's sister are the kind of man and sister the reader is going to follow through the rest of the book.

He read the fourth chapter. The fourth chapter was about the town. The fourth chapter was the kind of fourth chapter a reader reads in order to find out whether the town is a town the reader wants to read about. The town was a small town by a river. The man in the chapter had not been to the town in eleven years. The man on the porch had been to the town where the wedding had been, which was a different town by a different river, and which had been, by the fourth Sunday of August, the town the man had been meaning to write the friend a letter about since June.

He put the paperback down. He went inside and washed the peaches. He put the peaches in a bowl on the kitchen counter. He went back to the porch. He read the fifth chapter. The fifth chapter was about the man's sister waiting in the car. The fifth chapter was the chapter where the book decided, by the fifth chapter, that the book was going to be a book the reader was going to finish.

He read the fifth chapter and the sixth chapter, and the coffee cooled, and the porch went through one kind of light and then another, and by the time he closed the paperback for the second time the fourth Sunday of August was almost over. The paperback was on the chair beside him. The chair was the chair his wife sat in when his wife was on the porch. The chair was, by the fourth Sunday of August, the chair his wife was sitting in, and she had been sitting in it, in the way his wife sits in the chair when his wife has decided to let him read, since she had finished the newspaper at nine.

He handed her the paperback. She held it. She looked at the peach stain and the page twenty-eight crease and the corner of page twenty-eight that had been folded by the friend. She read the first sentence of the second chapter aloud, in the voice she uses when she has decided a sentence is a sentence the porch is going to give back to him when the porch has decided the sentence is finished being a sentence the porch is going to hold.

The sentence was not a difficult sentence. The sentence was the kind of sentence that looks ordinary on the page and is not ordinary when a person reads it in the right chair on the right morning. She read the sentence and handed the paperback back. She said, The library is going to want this back on Tuesday.

The library was going to want the paperback back on Tuesday. Tuesday was the first day of September. The paperback would be due on the first day of September, and the first day of September was the morning the tote bag was going to become a school bag, and the paperback was going to be on the bedside table for the next four days, and the bedside table was going to be the table the paperback sat on while the man and the man's wife read the rest of the book together at the rate the fourth Sunday of August had been quietly saving the porch for.

He would read the seventh chapter on Monday evening. She would read the eighth chapter on Monday after him. He would read the ninth chapter on Tuesday morning, after he had driven the paperback to the library and the tote bag had become a school bag. The book was the book. The Sunday was the Sunday. The tote bag was the tote bag. The paperback was, by the fourth Sunday of August, the paperback a tote bag had been carrying since June, and the paperback was, by the fourth Sunday of August, the paperback a man and a man's wife had decided to read together at the rate the fourth Sunday of August had been quietly saving the porch for.
