Ten days before the Booker Prize longlist is announced, the literary conversation gets very wide and very loud. It is also, in some quiet ways, very narrow. The lists this month are full of books that run to three hundred and fifty pages or more. The novels arriving in hardcover this summer, from Daniel Mason's *Country People* to Colson Whitehead's *Cool Machine*, are books built to be inhabited for weeks. They ask the reader to make a commitment, and the lists reward the books that get the commitment. This is the season of the long novel, and the season is doing what it is supposed to do.

But the long novel is not the only kind of book that earns a long afternoon. There is another form, quieter and more ancient, that has its own claim on the hours when nothing in particular is being asked of you. It is the short-story cycle. It is the kind of book you can read in one sitting, or three, or across the whole weekend, and which seems, in some quiet way, to know how long your afternoon is going to be before you do. The best short-story cycles are the books that taught American fiction how to write in shorter sentences, and Denis Johnson's *Jesus' Son*, published in 1992 and never out of print since, is one of the best of them.

## Why a Thirty-Four-Year-Old Book Still Matters

There is a class of literary books that never leave the backlist, but rarely get discussed as perennial titles either. They live in the used-book stores and the MFA syllabi. They are the books writers cite when they are asked which writers taught them how to write. They are not always on the bestseller list, and they are not usually at the front of the indie store. But they are in print, and they keep getting passed from one reader to the next. *Jesus' Son* is one of these. It has been in print for thirty-four years. It was a National Book Critics Circle Award finalist in 1992. It was adapted into a film in 1999. It is taught in MFA programs at Iowa, Johns Hopkins, Columbia, Michener, and a dozen other programs where the question of how short a short story can be is taken seriously.

The book is also, by any reasonable measure, one of the most influential American books of the last forty years. Denis Johnson died in 2017, and in the years since, the inventory of writers who cite him has only grown: the spare paragraph, the quiet paragraph, the paragraph that trusts the white space around it. The list of writers who learned something about compression from this book is longer than the book itself. Yet for all that influence, the book rarely comes up in the same breath as Marilynne Robinson's *Gilead* or Toni Morrison's *Beloved* when readers talk about perennial backlist titles. That is a small mistake, and the long afternoon is a good place to repair it.

## What the Book Understands About an Afternoon

*Jesus' Son* is a cycle of eleven short stories narrated by an unnamed character the other characters call Fuckhead, sometimes shortened to FH. He is a young man drifting through the rural Midwest and the small cities of the West in the early 1970s. He is in and out of addiction. He loses friends. He witnesses accidents. He has a vision of grace in a hospital waiting room that does not save him and that he does not entirely understand. The book is short. It can be read in two long sittings or one short one. The prose is spare, the dialogue is stripped to its working parts, the paragraphs often consist of a single sentence.

What the book understands about an afternoon is that the afternoon does not always know how long it is. Some afternoons stretch. Some afternoons end. Some afternoons are interrupted by something you did not see coming. The short-story cycle is the only literary form that respects this uncertainty. A novel demands a commitment. A short-story cycle makes an invitation. You can read one story and put the book down. You can read three. You can read five and lose track and find that the afternoon is gone and the book is still there, waiting, not because it needs to be finished but because the next story is also a beginning.

This is, in part, why *Jesus' Son* works so well in the long middle of July. The literary conversation right now is about which novels deserve three hundred and fifty pages of your attention. *Jesus' Son* is the kind of book that does not ask for three hundred and fifty pages. It asks for one page. Then another. Then a third. Then you look up and the afternoon is different and the book has done, in the smallest possible increments, something a longer novel could not have done in twice the space. It has entered your afternoon without rearranging it.

## The Prose Is the Argument

What Johnson learned, and what he teaches by example, is that a sentence can carry more weight when it has room to breathe. The book's most famous story, "Car Crash While Hitchhiking," opens with a paragraph that is also the entire moral premise of the cycle: the narrator is driving with a couple who have just hit something on the highway, and they have stopped to look, and the narrator watches them walk back toward whatever it is, and what he feels is a kind of tender awe at the way they keep going. That paragraph is also the entire reason the book works. It is spare, accurate, and final. It does not try to be more than it is. It does not try to make a point about life. It just describes what happens, in the order it happens, with the quiet grace of a person who has seen enough to be done with pretense.

This is the prose that taught a generation of writers how to write in shorter sentences. Not because shorter is always better, but because shorter, when it is honest, is often truer. The book is full of paragraphs that do more with less than most writers do with twice the room. It is full of dialogue that is just people talking. It is full of rooms that are just rooms. The effect, on a reader who has been spending the summer with three-hundred-and-fifty-page novels, is not that the book feels slight. It feels clear. The summer has been full of sentences, and here, suddenly, is a sentence that is doing exactly one thing, and that thing is the only thing it needs to do.

## A Book That Is Also a Reading Practice

The most interesting thing about *Jesus' Son* in the backlist conversation is that it is, in addition to being a book, a reading practice. You can teach yourself, in a weekend with this book, what most MFA programs take two years to teach. The first lesson is about leaving things out. The book tells you about a man, his friends, a couple of car wrecks, a hospital, a wedding, a few deaths, and that is all. It does not tell you about his childhood. It does not tell you about his parents. It does not tell you about the year before. It does not need to. The reader fills in what is missing with their own experience of being lost.

The second lesson is about rhythm. Johnson understood that a short-story cycle has its own music, and that the music is built from the spaces between the stories as much as from the stories themselves. The book has eleven stories and ten spaces. The spaces do the work. A reader who notices this starts to notice it in every other book they read, and the noticing is irreversible.

The third lesson is about trust. The book trusts the reader to understand what is happening without being told. The narrator does not explain himself. The book does not explain itself. The reader is invited, gently and without ceremony, to bring their own attention to the page, and to accept that what they bring is what the book will become. There is no better book to read on a long afternoon when you have finally stopped trying to be told what to think.

## Why This Belongs in the Long Afternoon

The argument this issue wants to make is small. It is not that *Jesus' Son* is better than the long novels of the summer. Some of those novels are very good. The argument is that the long afternoon has room for more than one kind of book, and that the short-story cycle is a form that the literary conversation, in its current concentration on length and prizes, has been quietly undervaluing. *Jesus' Son* has been in print for thirty-four years. It will be in print for thirty-four more. It is the kind of book that a reader can return to every summer for the rest of their reading life and meet a slightly different book each time.

If you have not read it, this is the right week to begin. If you have read it, this is the right week to return. The book is short. The afternoon is long. The combination is the entire argument. ## Worth Carrying

The literary conversation in the weeks before a prize list is full of talk about which books deserve three hundred and fifty pages of attention. The long afternoon is the right place to remember that some of the books that have changed American fiction most decisively are also the shortest ones on the shelf.
