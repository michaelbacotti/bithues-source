The second Saturday of August is, by every honest measure, the most quietly underserved morning of the literary calendar. The summer reading lists ended in the second week of July. The fall preview lists do not start until Labor Day. The Booker longlist dropped ten days ago and has, by now, hardened into the kind of structural argument that is no longer listening to outside input. The shortlist will not drop until September 22. The winner will not be named until November 9. Between this Saturday and the moment the shortlist arrives, there are six full weeks during which the literary conversation will, by every reliable pattern, keep arguing about the same thirteen titles it has already decided it intends to argue about. The second Saturday of August is the first morning inside that dead air.

This is the season the lists do not know how to curate. It is also, by every honest measure, the season the reading life has been quietly waiting for. The beach blanket is folded. The airport bookstore has stopped curating. The prize conversation has moved to a different room. The second Saturday of August belongs, by default, to the reader who has stopped reading for the season and started reading for the year.

What follows is a seasonal shelf for that morning. It is built around three books the long quiet stretch between the longlist and the shortlist has quietly been waiting for: a literary novel about a retired chef who inherits a small town and a second chance, the longest-awaited novel of the year from the author of *The Tiger's Wife*, and the Indie Next #1 pick for August about a former military dog walking across a broken land. None of them are on the longlist. None of them will be on the shortlist. None of them are packaged for the beach or the cookout or the plane. All three of them ask for the kind of reader the second Saturday of August quietly produces: a reader with a long morning, a slow afternoon, and a willingness to be changed a small amount by books that are not in the conversation yet.

## When Lemons Give You Life, by Anna Johnston

The book the second Saturday of August most quietly wants is a book that has, by every pre-publication signal, already been adopted by the readers who arrive early to literary fiction and who tend, by structural accident, to pick the books that win the second half of the year. *[When Lemons Give You Life](https://www.amazon.com/dp/006339734X/?tag=bithues-20)*, by Anna Johnston, publishing from William Morrow on the fourth of August, is one of those books. Johnston is the Australian author of *The Borrowed Life of Frederick Fife*, the 2024 novel that became a quiet international bestseller, that was acquired by Netflix for adaptation, and that made Johnston, by structural momentum, the writer readers started quietly waiting for. *When Lemons Give You Life* is the answer.

The novel is about a retired chef named Lemoine who has, by the first page, lost the taste for her own cooking. She has spent forty years inside a kitchen that was, by her own quiet admission, the only life she knew how to live. She has retired to a small town on the south coast of Australia. She has, in the retirement, discovered that the recipes she spent her life perfecting have stopped tasting like anything. The novel follows Lemoine across the slow recovery of a sense of flavor that the working years quietly burned out, and across the second chance the small town quietly offers, and across the family secret the recovery eventually surfaces. The book is, by every early review, funny in the way literary fiction is rarely allowed to be funny. The book is also, by Johnston's own structural choices, generous in the way literary fiction is rarely allowed to be generous. The book does not punish its protagonist for the life she lived. The book allows the life she lived to be enough.

The reason this book belongs on the second Saturday of August is that it is the kind of novel the long quiet stretch between the lists has, by structural design, been waiting for. It is a book about the long quiet stretch of a life after the loud stretch has ended. It is a book about a reader who has spent the summer reading for other people, and who has, by the second Saturday of August, quietly decided to read for herself. It is a book the longlist conversation will, by its own architecture, never see, because the longlist conversation does not, by its own eligibility rules, read novels published in Australia, and because the longlist conversation does not, by its own design, read novels that are, on their surface, this kind. Read it anyway. The book is the book. The book does not, by itself, mind.

## Sunrise, by Téa Obreht

The second book is the longest-awaited novel of the year, and the one the second Saturday of August has, by structural luck, the longest possible morning to read. *[Sunrise](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Sunrise+Tea+Obreht&tag=bithues-20)*, by Téa Obreht, publishing from Random House on the eleventh of August, is Obreht's third novel. It arrives ten days from publication as of this Saturday, and the literary pages have already begun the slow, attentive pre-publication work that Obreht's novels tend to quietly generate. Obreht is the author of *The Tiger's Wife*, which won the Orange Prize for Fiction in 2011 and made her, at twenty-six, one of the most-watched novelists in American letters. Her second novel, *Inland*, came out in 2019 and divided the readers who arrived to it expecting *The Tiger's Wife* and the readers who arrived to it expecting Obreht. Both kinds of readers have been waiting, since 2019, to see what she would write next. *Sunrise* is, by every pre-publication review, the answer.

The novel is set in a place called Sunrise, a ghost town in the American West that has accumulated a hundred years of arrivals and departures. Three lives move through it. A stranded young woman arrives, in the present, with no plan and no past she is willing to discuss. An early-twentieth-century gunslinger arrives, in the past, on a horse that has seen more of the country than the man riding it. A historical reenactor arrives, somewhere in between, and finds that the role he is rehearsing is the role the town has been waiting for. Obreht has built a novel that uses the structure of a ghost town to ask the question that ghost towns always quietly ask, which is the question of what happens to the people who stay after the people who left have stopped coming back. The novel is, on its surface, an adventure. The novel is, in practice, a meditation on the long quiet stretch of a place that has been written about more than it has been lived in.

The reason this book belongs on the second Saturday of August is that the novel is, by every structural choice, about the long quiet stretch of waiting. The book is not out yet. The book will be out in ten days. The book has, however, been read by enough early reviewers by now to be recommended in good faith, and the pre-order window is the right window to think about it in. Read the excerpt. Read the early reviews. Decide whether the voice is yours. If it is, pre-order. If it is not, set it aside until September, when the conversation will catch up with it, and the conversation will, by then, be the wrong reason to read it.

## Etna, by Paul Yoon

The third book is the one the indie booksellers have already decided is the book of August, and the one the longlist conversation has, by structural luck, the longest possible time to overlook. *[Etna](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Etna+Paul+Yoon&tag=bithues-20)*, by Paul Yoon, published by Scribner on the fourth of August, is the #1 Indie Next List pick for August, and it is the kind of book the indie booksellers reach for first when they want to recommend a novel that will not appear on any prize list before the year is out. Yoon has been a quiet, deliberate novelist for twenty years. He is the author of *Once the Shore*, *The Mountain*, and *The Hive*, three books that the literary conversation has always been slightly behind on, and that the readers who actually read them have always remembered. *Etna* is his fourth novel, and it is, by every early review, the most ambitious of the four.

The premise is one that the jacket copy will oversimplify, and that the novel itself treats with great seriousness. Etna is a former military dog. He has survived a war. He is, by some measure, the last of his unit. The novel follows Etna across a broken land of haunted people and haunted animals, and the voices that Yoon creates for both are the reason the book is on this shelf rather than on a thriller table. The dog does not narrate the book in the way a child narrator narrates a fable. The dog narrates the book the way a writer narrates a book when the writer is paying attention. The prose is patient. The prose is also, by every early review, the kind of prose a reader will remember for the rest of the summer, and possibly for the rest of the year.

The reason this book belongs on the second Saturday of August rather than on the September shortlist is that *Etna* is, by every structural choice, a book about the long quiet stretch of a journey after the loud stretch has ended. The war is over. The summer is almost over. The lists are pointing elsewhere. The dog is still walking. The book is, by every indication, the kind of book a reader will finish on Sunday and want to lend to someone on Monday, and the second Saturday of August is, for a reader, exactly the morning the lending quietly begins.

## A Note on Reading Order

If you read all three of these books in the next six weeks, read them in the order they are listed. *When Lemons Give You Life* first, because it is the warmest in voice and will reward a reader who has, by the second Saturday of August, started to feel the summer quietly ending. *Sunrise* second, because it is the longest and will reward a reader who has already cleared the noise out of the room. *Etna* third, because it is the quietest and will read, by the second Saturday of September, like a letter from the long quiet stretch to the year.

If you read only one of them this weekend, read *Etna*. It is the book that will still be on the bedside table in November, and it is the book the indie booksellers have already decided is the book of August.

If you read none of them this weekend, set the list aside. Read whatever else the long quiet stretch asks you to read. The shelf will still be here in two weeks, and the longlist conversation will, by then, have moved on to the shortlist speculation, and these three books will still be doing exactly what they have been doing all summer: accumulating readers in the long middle, waiting for the kind of reader that the second Saturday of August quietly produces.

## What This Week Is For

This week is for the books the longlist conversation has, by structural design, no time for. It is for the literary novel about a retired chef that the longlist eligibility rules quietly exclude. It is for the longest-awaited novel of the year that the longlist conversation is, by structural accident, three weeks behind on. It is for the indie bookseller pick that the longlist conversation does not, by its own architecture, see until the year is half over. It is for the books the second Saturday of August quietly produces.

It is also, not incidentally, for the reader who has decided the longlist conversation can wait until September. That reader has six full weeks of slow reading ahead. That reader has three books to read in the order they are listed, on the kind of mornings the second Saturday of August quietly produces. That reader is the reader the longlist will not be reading with. That reader is, by structural luck, the reader August has been quietly written for.

The calendar will turn. The shortlist will drop on September 22. The fall lists will arrive the week after. None of those events will, in the end, be as consequential as the books a reader chooses for herself on the second Saturday of a long August. Some books do not announce their value. They accumulate it. The three on this shelf have been accumulating all summer. They will still be accumulating in November, when the rest of the season's reading has been quietly forgotten.
