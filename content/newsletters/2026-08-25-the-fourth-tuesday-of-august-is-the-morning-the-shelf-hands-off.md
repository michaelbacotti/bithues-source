There is a fourth Tuesday of August every year, and the fourth Tuesday is structurally the last Tuesday the season has to itself. The first Tuesday was the morning the longlist conversation had stopped rearranging itself and the back wall of every bookstore had quietly started to move. The second Tuesday was the morning the year's most-considered novels were landing into a week nobody was watching. The third Tuesday was the morning the longlist had hardened into a closed argument and the quiet reader had begun to feel the cost. The fourth Tuesday is structurally different from any of the three. The fourth Tuesday is the morning the literary calendar has, by structural inevitability, handed the baton from August to September without telling the season the baton has been handed. The fall preview lists have not yet started to drop. The school calendars have. The Labor Day machinery has started to assemble itself on the horizon. The fourth Tuesday of August is the last Tuesday the summer has to itself, and the books the last Tuesday unlocks are the books the calendar has been quietly preparing the season to read.

What follows is a micro-season for that handoff. It is built around three books the last Tuesday of August is structurally built to read, and that the longlist conversation has, by structural timing, decided to leave for the week the longlist conversation has stopped curating. A 1972 Finnish novel about a grandmother and her granddaughter spending a summer on a small island, written in twenty-two short chapters that follow a single season. A 2014 Iowa novel about a woman who arrives at a town late in life and discovers the town has been quietly waiting for her, told in prose that moves at the pace of weather changing across a small parsonage yard. A 2021 Irish novel about a coal merchant who delivers coal to a convent in the winter of 1985 and discovers what the convent has been quietly doing to the women in its care. None of them are on the longlist. None of them will be on the shortlist. All three of them ask for the kind of reader the fourth Tuesday of August quietly produces: a reader who has stopped reading for August and started, by structural accident, reading for September.

## The Summer Book, by Tove Jansson

The book the fourth Tuesday unlocks is Tove Jansson's [The Summer Book](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=The+Summer+Book+Tove+Jansson&tag=bithues-20), the novel she wrote in 1972 in the long shadow of her own mother's dying, and the novel is, by every structural choice, the book the last Tuesday of August is built to read because the novel is, by every structural choice, about the way a Finnish summer ends. The novel is twenty-two short chapters that follow a single summer on a small island in the Gulf of Finland, and the chapters are spent between a six-year-old girl named Sophia and her grandmother, who is eighty-five and whose daughter — Sophia's mother — has been dead for three years. The grandmother and the girl argue about mosquitoes. The grandmother and the girl argue about whether a wave has arrived. The grandmother and the girl argue, in the structural register of a novel that trusts its reader, about everything that matters between an old woman and a small girl who has just lost her mother.

Jansson was the writer who spent most of her working life drawing the Moomins for the children of Europe, and the Moomins are, by every honest measure, the cartoons the longlist conversation has decided not to see, and the structural decision is the longlist conversation's loss. *The Summer Book* is, by every careful reading, the book the dying wanted the living to be able to read. The book is short. The book is patient. The book is written in prose that moves at the pace of a sail tilting on the water, and the prose is, by every honest measure, the prose the longlist conversation has been quietly forgetting how to read. The novel does not argue for itself. The novel gives the reader an island, and the island is the structural condition the last Tuesday of August is structurally built to return to. Read it this week because the week is the last week August has to itself, and the last week August has to itself is the week the longlist conversation has, by structural accident, stopped being able to curate.

## Lila, by Marilynne Robinson

The second book is Marilynne Robinson's [Lila](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Lila+Marilynne+Robinson&tag=bithues-20), published by Farrar, Straus and Giroux in 2014 and the fourth book in the series of novels set in the small Iowa town of Gilead, and the book is, by every structural choice, the novel the last Tuesday of August is built to read because the book is, by every structural choice, about a woman who arrives at a town late in life and discovers the town has been quietly waiting for her. Lila is the novel Robinson wrote about the wife of John Ames, the seventy-six-year-old pastor of *Gilead*, and the novel is the structural decision to tell the love story the original novel could only, by structural design, refer to. Lila is, by every honest measure, the book the longlist conversation has, by structural timing, decided to leave for the week the longlist conversation has stopped curating.

Robinson is the writer who has been quietly teaching the American literary backlist what patient attention looks like when the patient attention is, by the writer's structural choice, paid to a life that almost did not happen. *Gilead* is the book the attention of the reader has been asking for. *Housekeeping* is the book the body of the reader has been asking for. *Lila* is the book the reader who has been quietly accumulating late-summer patience has been asking for. The novel is short, by the trade pages' standards. The novel is unsparing, by the literary pages' standards. The novel is, by every structural choice, the book the fourth Tuesday unlocks because the fourth Tuesday is the morning a reader has stopped needing a novel to argue for itself, and *Lila* is the book that has, by structural design, decided not to argue for itself since the day the novel was conceived. Read it this week because the week is structurally built to read a novel about a woman who arrived, and the woman a reader has been quietly becoming in August is exactly the woman the novel has been quietly waiting for.

## Small Things Like These, by Claire Keegan

The third book is Claire Keegan's [Small Things Like These](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Small+Things+Like+These+Claire+Keegan&tag=bithues-20), published by Faber and Faber in 2021 and by Grove Press in the United States in 2022, and the book is, by every structural choice, the novel the last Tuesday of August is built to read because the book is, by every structural choice, about the moment a person decides to do the thing. Bill Furlong is a coal merchant in a small Irish town in the winter of 1985, and the novel is the morning Furlong delivers coal to the local convent and discovers what the convent has been quietly doing to the women in its care. Keegan is the Irish writer who has been quietly producing the kind of literary fiction that makes serious readers wonder why serious readers still read novels about doing the right thing when novels about the moment before doing the right thing can do so much more.

The reason *Small Things Like These* is the right book for the fourth Tuesday is that *Small Things Like These* is the book the conscience of the reader has been quietly asking for. *The Summer Book* is the book the attention of the reader has been asking for. *Lila* is the book the body of the reader has been asking for. *Small Things Like These* is the book the conscience of the reader has been asking for. Keegan writes sentences that move at the pace of a man walking across a frozen yard in the early morning, and the sentences are, by every careful structural reading, the kind of sentences that return reading to the part of the reader that the longlist conversation has been quietly forgetting. The novel is set in a season that is structurally the season the longlist conversation has, by structural accident, decided not to read about, and the structural decision is, by every honest measure, the structural decision the last Tuesday of August is built to make. Read it this week because the week is the last week August has to itself, and the last week August has to itself is the week a reader who has been quietly doing the right thing all summer is finally allowed to admit, in print, that doing the right thing is the only thing the reader has been quietly doing.
