## The Week the Longlist Settles Into Its Long Middle

By Thursday afternoon, the Booker Prize longlist has been the longlist for ten full days. The thirteen books have been set out face-up at the independent stores that still set books face-up. The podcasts have begun the slow argument that runs from now until the shortlist drops on Tuesday the twenty-second of September. The hardcovers have settled into their first full week on the front table. The early essays have been written, the surprise omissions have been litigated, and the conversation has begun the long, slow job of deciding what it actually thinks. The first Thursday of August is, by every honest measure, the morning the longlist conversation stops being a news story and becomes a season.

It is also, by structural luck, the morning the backlist conversation finally gets to do its work. The prize conversation is built to read books published in the United Kingdom and Ireland between October of one year and September of the next. It is structurally unable to read anything older. It is structurally unable to read anything published elsewhere. It is structurally unable to read the books that took a writer half a century to write, because half a century ago is not this eligibility window, and the books the writer was writing then are not the books the prize is judging now. The longlist, in other words, has a window, and the window is narrow, and the books that exist outside the window have always been invisible to it by design.

This is the week those invisible books belong to.

This year, one of the thirteen titles on the longlist is M. John Harrison's *The End of Everything*, a novel the Booker judges have finally, in the eleventh hour of Harrison's fifty-five-year career, decided is the kind of novel the prize should notice. The longlist conversation is treating it as a discovery. *The Guardian* called the novel a near-future vision from an SF master. The Booker's own page describes it as haunting, formidable, and the work of one of the very great writers alive today. The Independent and the TLS and the New Statesman have, in the past ten days, run features that read, to anyone who has been quietly paying attention for half a century, like a slow recognition of something the literary conversation has always known and has only just decided to say out loud.

The first Thursday of August is the week to read what the longlist conversation is structurally unable to read, which is to say the rest of Harrison's backlist, the four and a half decades of work that preceded the longlisted novel and that the longlisted novel is, in some quiet way, the late summation of. *Climbers*, his 1989 novel, is the book the longlist cannot see. *The Sunken Land Begins to Rise Again*, his 2020 novel, is the book the longlist conversation has not yet figured out how to fit into its argument about *The End of Everything*. *Signs of Life*, his 1997 novel, is the book the longlist conversation has, by some distance, the most thoroughly forgotten. Read all three before September. The Booker conversation will still be here when you are done. The chance to read Harrison without the longlist listening will not be.

## The Book This Moment Unlocks

The book the first Thursday of August most needs is a book the longlist conversation cannot see, and the reason it cannot see it is that the book was published in 1989, when the longlist conversation was busy noticing a very different set of thirteen novels and the literary Internet had not yet been invented. M. John Harrison's [Climbers](https://www.amazon.com/Climbers-M-John-Harrison/dp/057503632X/?tag=bithues-20) is thirty-seven years old this summer. It is also, by every honest measure, the best novel about rock climbing ever written, and one of the best novels about friendship, exhaustion, and the slow erosion of why a person does the things he does, ever written by anyone who has ever tried to write a novel about any of those subjects.

The book follows a small group of British climbers across the years they spend on the Yorkshire gritstone and the Scottish winter routes and the alpine north faces that the protagonist has been quietly working his way toward since the first chapter. The narrator-protagonist is a quiet, methodical, slightly broken man who climbs because climbing is the only thing he has ever done that does not ask him to be anything other than what he is. His friends are louder, more ambitious, more articulate about why they climb, and the novel is, in its quiet middle, the record of what happens when a man who has climbed his whole life slowly realizes that the climbing has stopped being enough. Robert Macfarlane, in a 2013 essay for *The Guardian* that accompanied the Gollancz reissue, called *Climbers* the best novel about rock climbing ever written, and then, correctly, noted that such a description drastically limits its achievement.

The novel does not announce its value. It accumulates it, the way a long pitch on a cold wall accumulates difficulty. The prose is patient. The prose is also, in places, almost unbearably precise about the specific kind of exhaustion a body feels at the end of a long route, and the specific kind of silence that descends on two climbers in a bothy bag at three in the morning when neither of them has the energy to perform the conversation the other one needs. There is a passage in the third chapter that is entirely about the sound a carabiner makes when it is clipped to a sling in cold weather, and that passage is, by itself, worth more than most novels published in any given year. Read it slowly. Read it on the kind of afternoon the first Thursday of August quietly produces.

## Why Climbers Often Fails at the Wrong Time

It is also, importantly, a book that often fails at the wrong time. *Climbers* is a book the literary conversation has, by some structural accident, mostly skipped. It has been continuously in print in the UK since 1989. It won the Boardman Tasker Prize for Mountain Literature. It was reissued by Gollancz in 2013 with a new introduction by Robert Macfarlane and a new afterword by the author. None of this has translated into the kind of mass readership that novels which win the major literary prizes tend to receive. The book has, by every measurable signal, been read for thirty-seven years by a small, devoted, deeply serious audience of readers who have been quietly recommending it to each other without the conversation ever quite catching up.

The reason *Climbers* often fails at the wrong time is that it is, structurally, the slowest novel Harrison has ever written. It does not have a plot in the way that novels are usually expected to have a plot. It has a series of climbs, and a series of friendships, and a series of small deteriorations, and a long slow middle in which nothing much happens on the page and everything much happens off it. The reader who picks it up in February, when the brain is sharper and the prose diet has been more demanding, will sometimes put it down after forty pages and conclude that the book is thin, mannered, all surface. That is a fair conclusion. *Climbers* is, in places, all surface. The novel has no interior monologue in the way that novels are usually expected to have an interior monologue. There is no protagonist whose feelings the reader is meant to follow. There is no epiphany at the end. There is no event that the book has been building toward. The book does not have a shape, and it does not want one. It wants to be read the way one reads a long route, which is to say one move at a time, without asking what the route is for.

In August, after a summer of contemplative reading, the reader who picks up *Climbers* is very often exactly the reader the book was written for. The brain wants prose that moves at the speed of a body on a wall. The brain wants prose that does not pretend to be telling a story. The brain wants a book that can be read in two long evenings and that, in those two evenings, will deliver a hundred sentences worth keeping. The reader who tried *Climbers* in February and put it down is, often, the reader who, on the first Thursday of August, picks it up again and realizes that the book was not the variable. The reader was.

## The Quiet Late Style

If *Climbers* is the door, [The Sunken Land Begins to Rise Again](https://www.amazon.com/Sunken-Land-Begins-Rise-Again-M-John-Harrison/dp/0575096357/?tag=bithues-20) is the room on the other side. Harrison's 2020 novel, published by Gollancz in a paperback reissue in late 2021, is the book that arrives, in the backlist of a longlist year, as the late-style companion to the longlisted novel the conversation has only just noticed. The book follows two haunted figures, a man named Shaw and a woman named Victoria, through a contemporary England in which the physical world has begun, in small, unsettling ways, to lose its hold on the laws the reader has been taught to expect it to obey. There are disappearances. There are flooded basements in towns that have never flooded. There are people who seem to have lost the capacity to remember what they were doing yesterday. The novel is, by every review, a haunted book. It is also, by every honest measure, a book that does not know what to do with the haunting, and that is the point.

What *The Sunken Land* adds to the *Climbers* argument is the question of what a backlist writer owes the reader when the writer has, in late career, lost interest in the kinds of questions the literary conversation still wants the writer to answer. The longlist conversation will want *The End of Everything* to be about climate. The longlist conversation will want it to be about Britain. The longlist conversation will want it to be a late-style summation of a writer who has, in his eighties, finally found the kind of large public subject his earlier novels refused. The longlist conversation will be wrong about this, in the specific way the longlist conversation is always wrong about writers who have been quietly accumulating readers for fifty years. Read *The Sunken Land Begins to Rise Again* instead, and the longlisted novel will, by September, be a different book than the longlist conversation is currently making it.

The third book on this shelf is the oldest backlist book of the three and the one the longlist conversation has had the longest to forget. [Signs of Life](https://www.amazon.com/Signs-Life-M-John-Harrison/dp/0312156561/?tag=bithues-20), published by St. Martin's Press in 1997 and now in a quietly kept Vintage paperback, is a novel about a future Britain that is close enough to the Britain of 2026 to feel almost documentary. There are toxic romances. There is recombinant DNA. There is a fable about genetic and emotional manipulation that the literary conversation of 1997 was not, by some margin, ready for. The book has been quietly waiting in the backlist for thirty years. The first Thursday of August is, by structural luck, the morning the backlist gets to start talking about it again.

## What This Week Is For

This week is for the writer the longlist conversation has finally noticed at the end of a career the conversation has, by its own architecture, mostly skipped. It is for the 1989 novel about climbing that won the Boardman Tasker Prize and that Robert Macfarlane called the best novel of its kind and that has been quietly accumulating readers for thirty-seven years. It is for the 2020 novel about a haunted England that the longlist conversation has not yet decided what to do with. It is for the 1997 novel about a future Britain that the literary conversation of its own decade was not ready for and that has been waiting, with the patience of a backlist book, for the conversation to catch up.

It is also, more quietly, for the reader who has decided that the longlist conversation can wait until September. That reader has seven weeks of slow reading ahead. That reader has three novels to read in the order they are listed, on the kind of afternoons the first Thursday of August quietly produces. That reader is the reader the Booker conversation, by its own design, will not be reading with. That reader is, also, by structural luck, the reader Harrison has been quietly writing for, in one form or another, since 1971.

A longlist is a single afternoon. A reading season is seven weeks. Some books do not announce their value. They accumulate it. The three on this shelf have been accumulating since before the longlist conversation existed. They will still be accumulating in November, when the prize has moved on, and the conversation finally belongs to the books again.
