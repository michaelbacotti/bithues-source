## The Condition

Tomorrow afternoon, at two o'clock London time, the Booker Prize judges will publish the longlist. By tomorrow evening, twelve or thirteen titles will have become the books the literary Internet is talking about, and the rest of the year's reading will, for a week or two, be reshuffled around them. The week we are in right now is the last one in which that has not yet happened. It is also, more than any other week of the summer, the one where a reader's choice is still her own.

There is a specific kind of quiet that arrives on the Monday before a list drops. It is not the quiet of June, when the season has not yet begun. It is not the quiet of late August, when the season is finishing and the prizes are still weeks away. It is the quiet of the in-between week, the last one before the conversation changes. The libraries are half-empty. The bookstore tables are stacked with the books the longlist is about to elevate into the spotlight. The book club has not yet picked its September title. The reviews of the books that will end up on the longlist have not yet been written, because the reviews of longlist books are, in most cases, written in the days after the longlist drops, not before.

This is the last day, in other words, on which a reader can choose a book without the lists making the choice easier or harder. By Wednesday, every conversation will be downstream of the longlist. By Friday, every reading recommendation will be a recommendation about a longlist book or a book that is being read in opposition to one. The next six weeks will be defined, in one form or another, by the prize.

Today is the day that still belongs to the reader.

## The Book This Moment Unlocks

The book the day before the longlist wants is a book the lists will almost certainly skip. It is a book that lives on the other side of the genre line the prize draws around itself every year. It is a book the judges are unlikely to read in serious quantity, because the judges are reading the books the prize is built to recognize, which is to say books that look like Important Books, not books that read like ones. The book this Monday wants is the kind of book a reader can finish in two sittings, on a long afternoon, with the door closed.

Silvia Moreno-Garcia's [The Intrigue](https://www.amazon.com/Intrigue-Silvia-Moreno-Garcia-ebook/dp/B0FWBBFLWK/?tag=bithues-20), published by Del Rey on July 14, is that book. It is a noir novel set in 1940s Mexico City about a twenty-nine-year-old con artist named Ulises Linares, who is down to his last few pesos, three years after his father and partner in crime died following a long illness. Ulises is dispatched to a small Veracruz town to separate a recent widow from a small fortune her late husband left her in a coffee-plantation investment. He arrives at the widow's hacienda. The widow is not what he expected. The town is not what he expected. The con, by the end of the first chapter, has begun to behave less like a con and more like a question Ulises is asking himself about the life he has been living.

The reason this is the right book for the last quiet Monday is not that the book is quiet. The book is loud. The book is full of money, of long con, of seduction, of the kind of low-grade moral arithmetic that lets a reader pass a long afternoon inside someone else's calculations. The reason this is the right book is that it is a book the longlist will, almost certainly, ignore. The Booker Prize almost never recognizes genre fiction, and Moreno-Garcia, by writing a novel that is recognizably noir in the mode of the paperback original, has built a book that lives below the prize's altitude line. That is the book's gift to a reader who still has one more quiet day. She gets to read it without the longlist noticing.

The book also rewards the kind of attention a Monday afternoon can give it. The chapters are short. The plot, while intricate, is paced for a single sitting. The Veracruz setting is specific enough to disappear into without making demands of the reader about her own life. By the time Ulises has finished the first stage of the con, the reader will have spent ninety minutes somewhere that is not the United States in July, and the longlist will not yet have happened, and the long afternoon will still be unfolding the way it is supposed to unfold.

## Two More Books That Earn This Moment

The second book is the one the longlist will also skip, and for the opposite reason. [Catch the Devil](https://www.amazon.com/Catch-Devil-Murder-Deception-Injustice-ebook/dp/B0FWBBFLWN/?tag=bithues-20) by Pamela Colloff, published by Hachette on July 14, is a New York Times bestseller, a piece of long-form true crime about a serial con man on the Gulf Coast who fabricated confessions and helped send at least one innocent man to death row. It is the kind of journalism the literary pages will, almost certainly, leave to the crime writers. It is also the kind of book a reader who has been reading slowly all summer is suddenly hungry for, because the prose is rigorous and the pace is fast and the moral center of the book does not blink.

Colloff has been one of this country's most consequential criminal-justice reporters for two decades. Catch the Devil is the book she has been writing toward for most of that time. It is rigorous without being bloodless. It is furious without being a sermon. It is the kind of nonfiction the literary pages do not always know what to do with, and the kind of nonfiction that the longlist conversation almost never makes room for. Read it on purpose today, before the longlist conversation takes the air out of the room.

The third book is the one the longlist will not notice because the longlist stopped noticing it decades ago. [Stoner](https://www.amazon.com/Stoner-York-Review-Books-Classics/dp/1590171993/?tag=bithues-20) by John Williams, first published in 1965 and reissued by NYRB Classics in 2003, is one of those backlist novels that has been quietly accruing readers for sixty years without ever becoming the kind of book that wins prizes. It is the story of a man named William Stoner who is born on a farm in Missouri, takes a literature class at the state university, becomes a teacher, marries badly, has a daughter, has a love affair, has a hard life, dies at sixty-one. There is no plot to speak of. There is no scene the reader will repeat at dinner. There is only the slow, patient accumulation of a single human life, written with the kind of attention that almost no novel being published in 2026 remembers how to write.

The reason this is the right book for the last quiet Monday is that it is a book the reader reads when she has stopped reading for prizes. It is the book she picks up on the day she has decided that the longlist conversation is a conversation she will, for the next week, politely decline to participate in. Stoner is the kind of book the lists have always missed, and will always miss, and that is fine, because the book has never needed the lists. The book has been finding its readers, one at a time, for sixty years. It will still be finding them in November, when the prize has moved on and the conversation finally belongs to the books again.

## What This Week Is For

This is the last week in which a reader can pick a book before the lists arrive to do the picking for her. Tomorrow the longlist drops. By Wednesday the conversation will be downstream of the prize. By September the conversation will be downstream of the shortlist. By November the conversation will be downstream of the winner. The next six months of literary reading will be organized, more or less, around the books the prize elevates and the books readers are reading in opposition to those books.

Today is the last day that is still organized around the reader.

Read the kind of book the lists do not see. Read the caper that lives below the prize's altitude line. Read the long-form journalism the critics do not know how to place. Read the backlist novel the prize has never had time for. Read whatever the long afternoon wants, before Tuesday afternoon makes the afternoon someone else's.

The point is not that the lists are wrong. The point is that the lists will arrive tomorrow, and a reader who wants to read her own reading for one more day has one more day to do it in. Use it.
