She had been saving the book for the first Sunday of August. She had been saving it since June, which was how long the book had been sitting on the bedside table in the small apartment she had lived in alone for the past eleven months. The book was a paperback. It was the color of an August morning, which was the color she had used, years ago, to paint the kitchen of her first apartment and which she had not, until this June, seen on a book. The spine had been broken in once, carefully, by the woman at the bookstore who had recommended it, who had also broken it in once at her own table before handing it across the counter, and who had said, "It asks for a slow week."

She had not had a slow week until the first Sunday of August. The week before had been the kind of week that requires the reader to leave the bedside table closed. There had been a long phone call with her sister. There had been a difficult day at the office that had run into a longer evening at the office. There had been a Saturday spent at the hardware store, choosing paint for a wall she was not yet sure she wanted to paint. By Saturday night she was tired in the particular way that makes the body refuse the chair and the eyes refuse the page. She had gone to bed at nine. She had slept, for once, without dreaming.

Sunday morning. The light came through the curtain the way it does in early August, which is everywhere at once. She woke at six-thirty, which was the hour her body had decided was the correct hour to wake on a Sunday. She did not reach for the book. She made coffee in the small pot and carried it to the window and stood with it for a while, looking at the building across the street, which she had been looking at for eleven months and which she had, by now, almost finished understanding. The window was open. The morning was warm.

She did not read on the porch. She read on the porch in the late afternoon, when the sun had moved around the building and the chair by the window was too bright. But she did not think about the porch yet. She thought about coffee. She thought about whether to make eggs. She made eggs.

She ate the eggs at the kitchen table and watched the light move across the wall in the way light moves across a kitchen wall on a Sunday in August, which is to say with the slow confidence of something that has been doing this for a long time and is not going to stop. The book was on the bedside table in the next room. She could see the corner of it through the door. The book was waiting the way a book waits when a reader has decided it deserves a particular morning, and the morning has arrived, and the reader has decided, in the meantime, that eggs are more important.

The eggs were not more important. The eggs were the way the morning had of postponing itself, the way mornings have of letting the reader discover, by midmorning, that the morning has been spent without her having read a single sentence. This is not a failure. This is how the slow week begins.

By ten she had washed the dishes. By eleven she had walked to the corner store and back for a newspaper she did not need. By noon she had sat in the chair by the window with the newspaper open on her lap and the coffee cold beside her, and she had read the obituaries first, which was a habit she had acquired in the past year and which she did not entirely understand. The obituaries were, almost without exception, the obituaries of people she did not know. She read them anyway. She read them the way one reads the back of a cereal box when one is not yet ready for what one is going to do next.

She thought about the book on the bedside table. She thought about the woman at the bookstore who had said it asks for a slow week, and who had been right, and who had known she would be right, and who had handed her the book with the broken spine as if she were handing her a key. She was not yet the right hand. She was the hand that holds the key in the pocket and walks around the apartment for an hour, deciding whether the door is really worth opening.

She did not open the door.

She opened the newspaper instead. She read an article about a town in Maine she had never been to. She read an article about a small library in Vermont that had been saved by the people of the town, who had decided, in the spring, that the building mattered to them more than the building's tax bill. She read an article about an actress she had loved in 1994 who had died on a Thursday. She read the article twice. She set the newspaper on the chair and made more coffee, and the second pot was not as good as the first, but it was Sunday, and Sunday forgives a lot of small failures of coffee.

She went for a walk at three. The walk was not a long walk. It was the kind that takes a person past the small bookshop on the corner and back again, without the person stopping, although she did slow down at the window the way one slows down at the window of any bookshop one has been a customer of for any length of time. There was a new book in the window. She did not need to know what it was. She needed only to see that the bookshop was still doing its slow work of curating for the season.

She came home. The book was on the bedside table. The cover was the color of an August morning. She stood in the doorway for a long time. She thought about the woman at the bookstore, who had been right about the slow week, and who had known it would not begin until the reader had decided to let it begin, and who had handed her the book anyway, because the handing is also part of the slow week.

She did not sit on the bed. She sat on the floor beside the bed, with her back against the wall, which is the position she had read in when she was eight and which she had been meaning to return to for some time. She opened the book. The first sentence was about a woman who arrives at a house she has not been to in a long time. The second sentence was about the light through the window. The third sentence was about a chair that the woman in the book had been keeping for the right morning.

She read the first chapter on the floor beside the bed, with her back against the wall, and she did not turn on the lamp, because there was enough August light in the room to read by, and because the slow week was, by then, a thing she had decided to let happen.

By the time she closed the book it was almost six. The light through the curtain had changed color twice. The coffee on the kitchen table was cold. The newspaper was still in the chair. She had not read anything in the newspaper she would remember. She had read the first chapter of a book she had been saving for the first Sunday of August, and the book had earned the saving, and the saving had earned the book, and the floor was where the slow week had decided to begin.

She put the book back on the bedside table. She would read the second chapter on Monday, which is also a kind of Sunday, if a reader has decided that the slow week is to be a slow week and not a Sunday.
