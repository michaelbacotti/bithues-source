## The Brain in Late July

There is a week, somewhere around the second half of July, when a season of slow reading starts to feel like a season of slow thinking. The long books have been long. The contemplative books have been contemplative. The river books and the porch books and the books about women learning to live differently in country houses have all done their work, and the reader who has been faithfully meeting them on their own terms suddenly notices that the brain is moving at the speed of grass. It is not that the books were wrong. It is that the books were a temperature, and the temperature has held for a month, and somewhere around now the reader needs a sharper sentence the way a sleeper needs an open window.

This is not a complaint about slow books. It is a description of a real reading condition, the one that arrives every year in the second half of July, when the brain has spent weeks reading prose that was content to be long, lyrical, reflective, or quietly argumentative, and is now hungry for prose that does something else. Prose that is short. Prose that is sharp. Prose that moves like the brain actually moves on a Tuesday afternoon in August, which is to say in fragments, in jumps, in leaps across topic and back, in sentences that begin in the middle of one thought and end in the middle of another. Prose that is, in a word, journalistic, and that does not apologize for being journalistic.

Renata Adler's [Speedboat](https://www.amazon.com/Speedboat-NYRB-Classics-Renata-Adler/dp/1590176138/?tag=bithues-20) is the right book for that condition. It was published in 1976. It was, in its way, forgotten for most of the next thirty years. It was reissued by the New York Review of Books in 2013 with a new afterword by Guy Trebay, and it has, very slowly, become one of those books that serious readers cite when they want to signal that they are serious readers, which is a dangerous thing for a book to become, because it can also become the kind of book that nobody actually picks up. It is a hundred and seventy-seven pages long. It can be read in three afternoons. It is the kind of book that, once you have read it, you keep trying to give away, because the experience of reading it is so specific that you want someone else to have had it, and the only way to do that is to hand them the book and wait.

## What Speedboat Actually Is

Speedboat is a novel. It is also not a novel. It is a series of short, numbered sections, some of them a sentence long, some a paragraph, some two or three pages, that follow a woman named Jen Fain through what is mostly a single year in New York. Jen is a journalist. She writes about, among other things, a fictional senator named Henry Krammer and a fictional war that runs in the background of the book and that nobody quite understands. She lives alone. She has a series of disastrous romantic encounters, most of them short, most of them comic in the way that only real romantic disasters are comic, with the comic edge being that she, like every other character in the book, is fundamentally unsuited to the situation she has gotten herself into. She goes to a lot of parties. She goes to a lot of funerals. She reads a lot of books and tells people what is wrong with most of them. She is brilliant, observant, and almost completely unable to narrate her own life in a way that adds up.

The book does not add up either, and that is the trick. Adler wrote the book in a style she called "a series of takes," and the structure is closer to a notebook kept over a year than to a novel with a plot. There is no through-line. There are events, and reflections, and observations about other people at parties, and long passages about newspapers and what newspapers used to be for. There is a section that is entirely about a typographical error in a magazine. There is a section that is entirely about a man Jen meets at a wedding. There is a section that is, against all reason, about a chicken. The reader who comes to the book expecting a novel will be confused for the first fifty pages and then will, probably around page seventy, stop expecting a novel and start expecting a very good friend who is talking at the reader at the speed of thought, and at that point the book becomes hard to put down.

This is the feature, not the bug. The reason Speedboat works in late July is precisely that it does not stitch itself together. The brain in late July, after a month of being asked to follow a hundred-year family saga or a three-hundred-page meditation on a single tree, sometimes wants prose that does not ask to be followed. It wants prose that hits. It wants sentences that arrive intact, without setup, without explanation, and that, in arriving, change the temperature of the room.

## Why It Often Fails at the Wrong Time

It is also, importantly, a book that often fails. Readers who pick it up in February, when the brain is sharper, when the prose diet has been more demanding, when the year still has shape, will sometimes put it down after forty pages and conclude that the book is thin, mannered, all surface. That is a fair conclusion. Speedboat is all surface. The novel has no interior in the way that novels are usually expected to have an interior. There is no protagonist whose feelings the reader is meant to follow. There is no epiphany at the end. There is no event that the book has been building toward. The book does not have a shape, and it does not want one. It wants to be read the way one reads a great magazine writer, which is to say for the sentences, and then for the next sentence, and then for the one after that.

This is also why Speedboat is famously a book that divides readers. There are the readers who, around page seventy, fall in love with it and keep it on the shelf forever. And there are the readers who, around page seventy, give it to a used bookstore. Both responses are reasonable. Both are evidence that the book is doing something specific. Most novels try to please as many readers as possible for as long as possible. Speedboat tries to please a particular reader at a particular time, and if that reader is not the reader who is reading it on that day, the book has nothing else to offer.

In late July, after a month of slower reading, the reader who picks up Speedboat is very often exactly the reader the book was written for. The brain wants sharper prose. The brain wants prose that does not pretend to be telling a story. The brain wants a book that can be read in three afternoons and that, in those three afternoons, will deliver a hundred sentences worth keeping. The reader who tried Speedboat in February and put it down is, often, the reader who, in late July, picks it up again and realizes that the book was not the variable. The reader was.

## Pitch Dark, Seven Years Later

If Speedboat is the door, [Pitch Dark](https://www.amazon.com/Pitch-Dark-Classics-Renata-Adler/dp/1590176146/?tag=bithues-20) is the room on the other side. Adler's second novel, published in 1983 and reissued by NYRB in 2013 with an afterword by Muriel Spark, picks up several of the threads from Speedboat and refuses to weave them. The book is again a series of short sections. The book is again about a woman, this time named Kate, who is a journalist, who is traveling, who is observing. But the book is darker, slower, less comic, and structurally even more willing to frustrate a reader who has come looking for a plot.

What Pitch Dark adds to the Speedboat argument is the question of what the fragmentary style is for when the surface is no longer comic. In Speedboat, the disjointedness feels like the texture of New York, of parties, of the way a journalist's brain works when she is between stories. In Pitch Dark, the disjointedness is the texture of grief, of travel in places where one does not speak the language, of the experience of not being able to stitch one's life together even in one's own head. The books are a pair. They argue with each other. They share a method but apply it to very different material, and a reader who reads them back to back in late July will end the second one with the sense that Adler was not writing fragments because fragments were fashionable. She was writing fragments because that is what the inside of attention actually looks like, and a novel that tries to stitch attention into a plot is, in some quiet way, lying about what attention is.

The afterword by Muriel Spark, who knew Adler and whose own novels are famous for being short and strange, is itself worth the price of admission. Spark writes about Adler in a way that suggests both writers understood that the modern novel's job is not to tell a story but to render a mind, and that minds do not tell stories. Minds wander, and the novel that wants to be honest about the mind has to wander with it.

## A Companion From Across the Aisle

The third book on the shelf this week is from a different tradition entirely, and it earns its place beside Adler because it asks the same question the journalist in Speedboat is asking, just from the other side of the notebook. [The Journalist and the Murderer](https://www.amazon.com/Journalist-Murderer-Janet-Malcolm/dp/0679731830/?tag=bithues-20), by Janet Malcolm, first published by Knopf in 1990 and now in its Vintage paperback, is a hundred and sixty pages about a lawsuit. A writer named Joe McGinniss sued the journalist Janet Malcolm for libel after she wrote about him in a New Yorker piece. Malcolm took the case seriously, and the book she wrote is not a memoir of the lawsuit. It is a book about what journalists do, and what writers do, and what the contract between a writer and a subject really is, and whether that contract is honored or merely performed. The book is fragmentary in a way that is closer to the rhythm of an actual case file than to the rhythm of an actual novel, and the reader who has just come off Speedboat and Pitch Dark will recognize the texture immediately. The book is short, sharp, slightly uncomfortable, and willing to follow a thought past the place where a less rigorous writer would have stopped. It is also the kind of book that, once read, changes how a reader reads everything else that purports to be true. Read it after Adler. The two books together are a small education in how prose moves when prose is being honest with itself.

## What This Week Is For

This is the week to read a book that does not pretend to be larger than a hundred and seventy-seven pages. The long books are waiting for September, when the brain will be sharper and the light will be different. The contemplative books are waiting for August, when the heat will break and the reader will, again, want prose that is willing to hold a single thought for a hundred pages. But this week, in the quiet middle of late July, with the lists having gone home for the season and the chair holding most of the day's attention, the right book is the one that moves at the speed of the reader's actual mind. It is a backlist book. It is a New York book. It is a book that, if you have been hearing about it for years and never picked it up, is finally ready to be picked up. The season has made room for it. The reader has, too.

A reading light is not romantic, but neither is eye strain. A book that comes back into print after thirty years of quiet is not, on its own, an event. But the combination of a reader who has been patient all month and a book that has been patient for thirty years is, occasionally, the entire point of late July.

Speedboat. NYRB Classics. One hundred and seventy-seven pages. The right book for this week.
