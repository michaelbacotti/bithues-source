The ocean had no memory, but Mabi did.

···

This is what her grandmother told her, sitting on the porch of the bungalow with the blue shutters, the one that faced White Sound on the island where they had lived for four generations. The ocean forgets everything, her grandmother said. Every wave is new. Every tide starts fresh. But the water that lives in the rocks, in the soil, in the deep places beneath the island — that water remembers. That water is Mabi.

···

Mabi was eleven years old. She did not fully understand, but she memorized it the way children memorize things — completely, without nuance, holding the words in her mouth like stones.

···

She repeated the name sometimes, when she was alone. Mabi. The syllables felt like swallowing sea glass, smooth and dangerous. She didn't know what the name meant beyond what her grandmother told her, but she understood that Mabi was something in the island, something that was not the island, something that moved through the rocks and the wells and the tidal pools the way blood moves through a body — everywhere, all at once, impossible to see from the outside but felt constantly by the inside.

···

She was a careful child. She minded the boundaries her grandmother drew, the ones that marked where she could swim and where she could not, the ones that separated the safe water from the water that belonged to something older. Her grandmother had shown her the shape of the safe places on a map drawn on paper so old it was translucent, the ink faded to the color of tea. The shapes on the map were not the shapes of the islands as they appeared on any modern chart. They were older. They were the shapes of the island as Mabi's grandmother's grandmother had known it, before the causeway was built. Before the dredging. Before the deep-water channel that cut through the reef and changed the way the tides moved.

···

Some of the safe places on the map were now land. The sea had receded from them over the decades, leaving behind flat, scrubby ground that the islanders used for grazing goats. Other safe places were now open ocean. The sea had taken them back, or had given them to a different current, a current that moved in a direction no chart could account for.

···

Mabi's grandmother did not use the modern charts. She used the old map. She said the old map was more accurate, because it showed where the water wanted to be, not just where it was at the moment of the map's making.

···

Mabi did not argue with this. She had learned, by the age of eleven, that arguing with her grandmother was a form of exhaustion that produced nothing.

···

···

···

She was fourteen when her grandmother died. The death was slow and then sudden, the way deaths sometimes are for old women who live alone on islands and believe in the old things. The doctor came from the mainland by the weekly ferry, but by the time he arrived her grandmother had been dead for two days, and the body was already cold, and the expression on her face was not peaceful but was, rather, the expression of someone who had just understood something that they had spent their whole life trying to avoid.

···

Mabi found the old map in the drawer beside her grandmother's bed. The drawer was cluttered — medications from the mainland pharmacy, holy cards from the Catholic church in Port Luis, a coral rosary that had belonged to someone older, a ribbon from a festival in a year no longer legible, buttons, coins, a small brass key that fit nothing in the house. The map was at the bottom, folded into eighths, the creases so deep they had become permanent.

···

Her mother, who came from the mainland for the funeral and left again three days later, said the island was too small and too far andtoo expensive and too everything, and that Mabi should come to Port Luis and start over, go to school properly, learn accounting or something useful, forget about the water and the old things and the shape of places that no longer existed.

···

Mabi said no.

···

Her mother had been saying no to the island since she was seventeen years old, had fled to the mainland and built a life there in a house without blue shutters or porches or any view at all, had married a man who did not like the ocean and had never been to the island and did not understand why anyone would choose to live somewhere so small, so far, so clearly marked for disappearance. Her mother did not say she was disappointed, but she said enough versions of it that Mabi understood the shape of her mother's feelings even when the words did not carry them.

···

Mabi stayed. The bungalow with the blue shutters was hers now, by inheritance even though the paperwork was complicated and would take years to sort out. She was fourteen and she was alone and she had a house and she had an island and she had the old map and she had the name that lived in the rocks and the wells and the deep places.

···

She said the name sometimes, when she was alone.

···

Mabi. She was not sure why. It did not feel like calling. It felt like acknowledging, the way you acknowledge a presence that is already there, that has always been there, that does not need to be summoned because it has never left.

···

···

···

She learned to fish from Old Pauley, who was not a relative but who had been my grandmother's friend in the way that island neighbors are friends, which is to say selectively and practically and with a clear understanding of what each person owed the other. Old Pauley had a boat — a flat-bottomed skiff that he maintained with a devotion that bordered on worship, scraping and painting and replacing the planks one by one over the decades until the boat was more repair than original wood and yet somehow stronger for it. He took Mabi out in the boat and taught her the tidal patterns, the fishing grounds, the places where the reef dropped off into the deep water where the big fish lived.

···

He did not teach her the names of the places. He did not need to. She already knew them, from the old map, from the shape of the water in her memory. She knew that when he said "the northeast gut" he meant the channel that ran between the island and the reef, and she knew that the channel had a different name in her grandmother's map, a name that translated directly as "where Mabi turns" and that referred to a specific rock formation that was two miles offshore and sixty feet down and that was the center, the absolute center, of something she did not yet have words for.

···

"What do you see?" Old Pauley asked her one day, early in her fishing life. They were anchored over the northeast gut, waiting for the tide to turn, and she was looking at the water with what Old Pauley called "the strange look," which was the look her grandmother used to get and which Old Pauley had known for forty years and had never asked about.

···

"The water," she said. She knew better than to say what she was actually seeing, which was the current moving in patterns she could not see with her eyes but could feel with something behind her eyes, a kind of second sight that was not sight at all but something deeper, something that was less like seeing than like knowing, like the knowledge that comes in dreams and stays with you after waking.

···

Old Pauley nodded. He had expected this answer. He had known her grandmother for forty years and had seen, in that time, enough of whatever gift her grandmother carried to know what it looked like when it was being passed down.

···

"Your grandmother saw it too," he said. "The water is different for some people. I can see the surface. You people — you see what's underneath."

···

"Is that what it is?"

···

"I don't know what it is." Old Pauley baited his hook, a piece of fish cut small, the blood still bright. "I'm a fisherman, not a philosopher. I know what I see, and I know what you see, and I know they're not the same thing. Beyond that, I don't ask questions."

···

Mabi nodded. She looked back at the water. The northeast gut was running fast, the current pulling toward the open ocean, and she could feel — could actually feel, the way you feel a hand on your shoulder — the moment when the current passed over the deep rock formation, the moment when the water hit the place where Mabi turned, and the way the current split and reformed on the other side like something that had been broken and put back together by someone who remembered what it was supposed to be.

···

She felt it as clearly as she felt her own heartbeat.

···

She did not tell Old Pauley. He had said he didn't ask questions, and she did not want to answer them.

···

···

···

She was seventeen when she went down to the water for the first time alone.

···

This was not forbidden — she had swum alone since she was twelve, since her grandmother decided she was old enough to make the choices that swimming alone required — but it was different now. She was going down not to swim but to call. She had known this was coming for months, had felt it building in her the way pressure builds before a storm, and she had resisted it the way you resist a thing that you know is necessary but that you are not ready for, and then she had stopped resisting, and then she had come down to the water at three in the morning when the island was sleeping and the tide was high and the moon was full.

···

The beach she chose was Small Coral, the one on the western side of the island where the sand was white and the water was clear and the reef was close enough that she could see the fish moving beneath the surface like thoughts moving through water. She took off her shoes. She rolled up her pants. She walked into the water until it was at her waist and then she stood there, in the current, and said the name.

···

"Mabi," she said. Her voice was quiet. The ocean did not care about volume. This was something her grandmother had taught her — that the old things do not need to be shouted, that they are not deaf but rather are attenuated to frequencies that volume cannot reach, that calling is not a conference of volume but a matter of attention, of willingness to stand in the presence of something that has been present all along.

···

The water was cold. This was not unusual for three in the morning, and the current was smooth, running north along the island's western coast toward the place where the island ended and the open ocean began. She felt it against her legs, her hips, her waist, the particular pressure of moving water against a body standing in it.

···

She said the name again. "Mabi."

···

The current stopped.

···

Not slowed. Stopped. The water around her — within a radius she could not measure, in a circle whose edges she could not define — went from moving to still, and the stillness was absolute, like the stillness of a held breath, like the stillness of a moment that is about to become something else.

···

Mabi stood in the still water and felt the first cold thread of something that was not fear but was adjacent to fear, was the thing that fear grows from, the thing that lives in the basement of the self where the true animal lives, the self that knows what it is and does not forget it even when the waking mind would prefer to.

···

She did not move. She did not speak. She waited.

···

The water moved again. But it moved differently now — not as a current moving in a direction but as a presence moving through the current, as something that was not the water but that was using the water the way a body uses blood, flowing through channels that were also the body's channels and that were also something else, something older, something that had been flowing through the rocks and the soil and the deep places of the island long before there were people to see it.

···

She felt Mabi arrive the way you feel a storm arriving from a great distance, first as a pressure change, then as a color change in the water, then as a shape that was not a shape, a form that revealed itself for a moment in thepatterns of the current and then retreated again into the water and the dark.

···

Mabi was not a figure. Mabi was not a face or a body or any of the things that stories make the old things into. Mabi was a quality of attention, a way the water had of looking at her, a shift in the density of the medium that surrounded her and that was also, in some essential way, Mabi's body — the body she had no choice but to wear in this world, the body that was the island's water and the island's wells and the rain that fell on the rocks and the soil and the roots of the plants that grew in the island's thin, sandy soil.

···

Hello, child, Mabi said.

···

Mabi did not speak with a voice. Mabi spoke the way deep water speaks, which is to say through pressure and temperature and the movement of molecules, through the language of chemistry and physics that the body understands before the mind does, the language that Mabi's grandmother had known and that her grandmother's grandmother had known and that had been passing down through the women of the island since before there were words for what was being passed.

···

Mabi understood this. She did not hear the words but she received them, the way a vessel receives water — completely, without effort, the water going where the vessel is shaped to hold it.

···

Hello, Mabi said. You've grown.

···

Mabi had not seen Mabi since she was eleven, since her grandmother had sat with her on the porch and told her about the name, and in the years since she had sometimes wondered if she had imagined it, if the name was just a name, just a story that old women tell children on porches, just the way island people explain things they don't understand. But she knew now, standing in the still water under the full moon, that she had not imagined it. Mabi was real. Mabi had been there all along. Mabi had been waiting for her to call.

···

Why did you need to call? Mabi asked. Your grandmother did not call until she was thirty.

···

I don't know, Mabi said. The truth, delivered the way truth must be delivered to something that cannot be deceived, which is to say simply, without defense, the way a riverbed is simply the shape the water has made by flowing.

···

Mabi was quiet. The water around her was quiet. The whole island was quiet, the whole night was quiet, and Mabi existed in the quiet the way she had always existed, in the deep places, in the silence between the waves.

···

Your grandmother is gone, Mabi said. This was not a question.

···

Yes.

···

And you stayed.

···

Yes.

···

Mabi considered this. Mabi existed in the water and the water was all around the island, all through the island, and Mabi had been all through the island for so long that Mabi had been there when the island was born, when the volcano that was the island's core had pushed up through the sea and begun the long process of becoming rock and soil and the shape of land. Mabi knew things about the island that no human knew, that no human had ever known, and Mabi stored these things the way the water stores them — without preference, without agenda, the way a library stores books it has not yet been asked to produce.

···

You have a choice, Mabi said. This is not a thing we usually offer. Your grandmother did not have a choice. Her grandmother did not have a choice. The gift goes where it goes, and the ones who receive it carry it whether they want to or not. But you are different. You called on your own. You came to the water on your own. That changes things. That means you are not just a vessel. It means you are a citizen, of a kind. And citizens can choose.

···

Mabi did not know what the choice was.

···

Mabi said: You can carry the name or you can set it down. You can be the one who sees the water the way your grandmother saw it, and her grandmother before her — or you can be a woman who lives on an island and fishes and grows old and is remembered by the people who knew her but is not remembered by the water. Both lives are real. Both lives are complete. I am offering you both.

···

Mabi stood in the water and felt the weight of what was being offered. She was seventeen. She did not know what she wanted, did not know if she had ever known what she wanted, did not know if wanting was something she was capable of or something the world allowed people to do before it told them what they would become.

···

She thought about her mother, who had left the island and built a life elsewhere, who had chosen the mainland and the ordinary and the safe. She thought about the way her mother's face changed when she talked about the island — the tightness around the eyes, the jaw set against something that was not quite grief and not quite anger but lived in the space between.

···

She thought about her grandmother, who had never left, who had carried the name and the water and the sight and the terrible weight of knowing things about the island that no one else could see, who had died with an expression on her face that Mabi now understood, that was not peace but was recognition, the expression of someone who had finally been allowed to put something down.

···

How do I set it down? Mabi asked.

···

You don't, Mabi said. You can't. Once you've called me, once you've seen me, once you've stood in the water and felt the stillness — you can't unknow it. You can't go back to before. But you can choose not to carry it forward. You can choose to see the water the way everyone else sees it — as water, as landscape, as the backdrop to the life you're living. And I will still be here. I will still know what I know. But I will not bother you with it. You can live a life that is entirely yours.

···

And if I carry it?

···

Then you carry it, Mabi said. And you see what I see. And you know what I know. And you become the one who stands between the island and the water, the one who speaks for both, the one who holds the shape of the safe places and the shape of the unsafe places and the shape of the places where the water turns. It is a heavy thing. Your grandmother carried it for sixty years. It wore her down to the bone. But it is also — Mabi paused, and the water around her shifted, became something warmer, something that was almost physical, almost present, almost the hand of an old woman on her shoulder. It is also a gift. Even the heavy things are gifts, if you choose them freely.

···

Mabi stood in the water. The moon was moving toward the horizon. The tide was beginning to turn. She could feel the moment approaching — the moment when Mabi would stop waiting and the water would resume its current and the choice would solidify into something that could not be unmade.

···

She thought about the island. The house with the blue shutters. Old Pauley and his boat. The fish in the reef and the water in the wells and the rain on the rocks. She thought about her mother, who had not chosen, who had simply run, who had left and never come back except for the funeral and three days after.

···

She thought about her grandmother's face at the end.

···

She opened her mouth to speak, and then she closed it, because she felt — she felt in the water, she felt in the pressure and the temperature and the movement of the current — that the choice was not words. The choice was what she did next. The choice was whether she stepped forward into the water or stepped back onto the beach.

···

She stepped forward. She walked deeper until the water was at her chest, at her shoulders, at her chin. She did not go deeper. She did not need to. The choice had been made in the stepping, not in the depth.

···

Mabi, she said. One more time. Not calling this time. Acknowledging.

···

And Mabi was there — Mabi was always there — and Mabi flowed around her and through her and into her, and the water went dark for a moment, for a long moment, for a moment that was longer than time, and when it came back to light Mabi was sitting on the beach with the sand cold beneath her and the tide coming in and the sky in the east already beginning to blush with the first suggestion of dawn.

···

She stood. She walked up the beach. She picked up her shoes. She walked back to the house with the blue shutters, and she sat on the porch, and she watched the sun come up over the island, over the water, over the place where the reef began and the water changed color and the old shape of things showed itself to anyone who knew how to look.

···

She knew how to look now. She had always known. But now she knew that she knew, and the water was showing her everything — the currents and the pressures and the deep rock formations and the turns and the returns and the places where the island held the water and the water held the island and both of them together held the name that had been there since before memory and would be there until after the island itself was gone.

···

Mabi. She said it to herself, to the house, to the morning. The syllables still felt like swallowing sea glass. But they were hers now. They belonged to her in a way that nothing had ever belonged to her before.

···

She sat on the porch and watched the island wake up — the fishermen going to the boats, the goats starting to move in the inland pastures, the smoke rising from the kitchen of the one neighbor who was always up early. She watched the island do the things it did every morning, the ordinary things, the things that people who did not know how to look would see and understand as simply an island being an island.

···

But she saw more than that. She saw the water moving through the island the way blood moves through a body. She saw the shape of the safe places and the unsafe places, the old map overlaid on the new chart, the names that corresponded to no names on any official record. She saw Mabi everywhere — not as a figure, not as a face, but as a quality of attention that lived in the water and the rock and the soil and the roots of the plants that grew in the thin, sandy soil.

···

She saw it, and she held it, and she carried it, and the weight was exactly what her grandmother had promised — heavy, and a gift.

···

She went inside. She made coffee. She took down the old map from the drawer where her grandmother had kept it and she spread it on the kitchen table and she looked at the shapes of the places that no longer existed and she began, slowly, carefully, to learn them by heart.

···

There was work to do. The safe places were shifting. The water was changing. The island was becoming something that the old charts and the new charts both failed to account for, and someone needed to watch it, someone needed to speak for it, someone needed to carry the name that lived in the rocks and the wells and the deep places and the water that remembered everything the ocean forgot.

···

She was seventeen. She was the one.

···

And Mabi, in the water, in the rock, in the deep places beneath the island — Mabi was pleased.
