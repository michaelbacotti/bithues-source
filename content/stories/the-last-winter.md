Thomas had two months of flour when November came. Not enough to last the winter — not if the winter was long, and the winter of 1888 was going to be long, Thomas knew this from the way the sky looked in October, from the way the cattle had been acting, from the particular quality of the cold that had settled into the valley in the last week of October and had not left.

···

He had a wife. He had a daughter who was four. He had a son who was six. He had land that was his by government document and by the sweat of his own labor, land that he had broken with his own hands, land that was not going to feed his family if the flour ran out in February and the snow was still on the ground.

···

He had made a decision in November. He would not speak of it until April.

···

··· ···

···

The first snow came early, which everyone said was a sign of a hard winter, which everyone knew already. Thomas watched it fall and calculated distances in his head: the nearest neighbor was seven miles. The nearest town was fifteen. The trading post where he bought flour was twenty-two, and the road to it would be impassable by December and possibly by late November.

···

He had two months of flour. He could stretch it if he rationed, if he and Sarah and the children ate less than they needed, if they were very careful and very lucky and the winter was not too long.

···

He did not believe they would be that lucky.

···

··· ···

···

In the third week of November, Thomas drove to the trading post. The road was still passable — barely. He bought what he could afford, which was not enough. The trader told him there would be no more supplies until spring. The trader told him to be careful. The trader told him what everyone told everyone in November in Montana, which was to hope for an early thaw and to prepare for a late one.

···

Thomas drove home. The flour was in the wagon. The sky was the color of a gun barrel. He had made a decision.

···

He would not speak of it until April.

···

··· ···

···

The first month of winter was the hardest on the children. They didn't understand why there was less food than before. They cried, and Thomas held them, and Sarah held him, and no one said what they were thinking because saying it would make it more real than it already was.

···

Thomas fed the cattle what he could spare, which was less than they needed. He checked the fences in the cold, which was not cold enough yet, which was the wrong kind of cold — the cold that comes before the real cold, the cold that is a warning and not a statement. He knew what was coming. He had seen it in the sky in October. He had seen it in the cattle. He had seen it in the way the birds had left early and not come back.

···

He had made a decision. He had made it in November, when the flour was two months and the road was still passable and the choice was still theoretical.

···

Now it was December, and the choice was not theoretical, and he fed his family what he had and he did not speak of what he had decided.

···

··· ···

···

On the twenty-third of December, Thomas walked into the barn in the middle of the night and shot three of his six cattle. He did it quickly, without hesitation, with the particular efficiency of a man who has had a month to prepare for exactly this moment and who knows that hesitation would make it worse.

···

He butchered them in the cold. He hung the meat in the shed. He buried the remains in the snow where no one would see them until spring.

···

Sarah did not ask questions. She had known, since November, that this was coming. She had known what he had decided. She had held the children and she had rationed the flour and she had not asked questions, because asking would not change anything and the silence was easier than the conversation.

···

The children asked about the cattle. Thomas told them the truth, in the way that children needed to hear it: that sometimes in winter, animals died, that this was how the world worked, that the meat was food and the food was survival and this was what people did when the world asked them to do hard things.

···

They were not satisfied with the answer. They were six and four. They would not be satisfied with any answer.

···

Thomas did not offer another one.

···

··· ···

···

January was the coldest month anyone could remember. Thomas did not know if this was true or if it only felt true, if the cold was actually historical or if it was simply the cold he was living through, which is always the worst cold, which is always the cold that matters because it is the cold that is here.

···

He fed his family on beef and flour and whatever Sarah could preserve from the root cellar. The root cellar was cold but not cold enough to freeze everything, and the potatoes and the turnips and the onions lasted longer than he had expected. The cattle were fewer now, which meant less feeding, which meant the hay would last through February as long as nothing went wrong.

···

He checked the hay every day. He checked the fences every day. He checked the children's faces every day, watching for signs of hunger that went beyond what the food could address, watching for the particular pallor that came from not eating enough.

···

They were thin. All of them were thin. Thomas was thinner than he had been in his life, which was saying something, because Thomas had been a lean man before November and was now something else — a man who had subtracted himself from his own body in order to keep his family alive.

···

··· ···

···

February came. The cold broke for three days in the second week, which was the worst thing that could have happened, because it made everyone believe the worst was over and Thomas had to remind them that February was February and March could still bring snow and the roads could still be impassable and the flour was not going to last another month if they were not careful.

···

He was right, of course. The cold returned. The snow came back. The last of the flour was gone by the twentieth of February, and Thomas fed his family on meat and root vegetables and the particular will that had kept him going since November, since the decision, since the night he walked into the barn and did what the winter required.

···

Sarah asked him, on the twenty-fifth of February, whether he thought they would make it.

···

He said yes. He did not know if he believed it. He said it because it was what she needed to hear, and because saying it was a form of will — a way of speaking the future into existence, of forcing the universe to comply with what he needed to be true.

···

··· ···

···

The thaw came in April. Not early, not late — on schedule, or close to it, the way thaws come in Montana, which is to say suddenly and completely, over the course of three days, with the snow disappearing into the ground and the cattle coming out of the barn and the world resuming the shape it had been before the cold.

···

Thomas drove to the trading post. He bought flour. He drove home. He fed his family a meal that was not rationed, that was not measured, that was simply food in the quantity that hunger required.

···

The children ate. They did not ask about the cattle that were not there. They had learned, as children learn, that some things were not to be asked about, that some silences were to be respected even when they were not understood.

···

Thomas ate. He sat at the table with his family and he ate and he did not speak of what he had done.

···

He had decided, in November, that he would not speak of it until April.

···

April had come.

···

He still did not speak of it.

···

Some things, once done, are better left in the silence where they happened. The winter had required something. He had provided it. The family was alive. The children were thin but alive. Sarah was tired but alive. The land was still his. The future was still possible.

···

What he had done in that winter was his. It was not for telling. It was not for explaining. It was for carrying — for carrying forward, for the rest of his life, the knowledge that he had done what the world asked him to do, and that the world had not thanked him, and that the world had not needed to thank him, because the world does not thank people for surviving.

···

It simply lets them survive.

···

And that, Thomas thought, watching his children eat, was thanks enough.

···

Was enough.

···

Was what there was. *End of story.*

*End of story.*
