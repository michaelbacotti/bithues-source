The neighborhood had changed. This was the first thing Maya noticed when she came to photograph it — not the changes that were visible, the new roofs and the updated siding and the cars that were newer than the cars she remembered from her own childhood visits, but the changes that were missing. The lawns were too clean. The yards were too flat. There were no garden beds, no vegetable plots, no flowers growing in the spaces between the house and the street.

···

Her project, the one she had been working on for two years, was called *The Last Gardens*. She traveled to places that had once been known for their gardens — not famous gardens, not tourist destinations, but neighborhood gardens, the kind that people planted in the plots of land between their houses and the street, the kind that made a block look like a block and not like a strip mall. She had photographed forty-three neighborhoods in twelve states. She had documented the slow disappearance of the vegetable garden, the flower bed, the patch of earth that someone had decided to tend instead of pave.

···

The neighborhood she was in now was in Ohio, in a small city near the river, in a part of the country that she knew mostly from driving through it on the way to somewhere else. The houses were 1950s and 1960s, the kind of houses that had been built quickly after the war to accommodate the families that were forming then, the families that had planted gardens because that was what you did, because the soil was there, because the houses came with yards and the yards were waiting for something to grow in them.

···

Maya had a list. She had made the list from historical photographs, from census records, from the oral histories that people had recorded and uploaded to the internet in the years before the internet became what it was now. The list told her that in 1962, on this block alone, there had been twenty-three gardens. She had found seven that still existed in some form — small gardens, half-gardens, gardens that had been reduced to a few plants in the narrow strip along the foundation. She had photographed all seven. She had two more to go.

···

Mrs. Okafor was her last appointment. She had called ahead, the way she always did, and Mrs. Okafor had agreed to let her come and photograph and talk, and Maya had gotten in her car and driven to the house, which was at the end of the block and which looked, from the street, like every other house on the block except for the garden that surrounded it on all sides.

···

···

···

The garden was not what Maya expected. She had been expecting a vegetable garden, maybe — the kind of organized beds that she had seen in the other gardens she had photographed, the kind that were remnants of a time when growing your own food was not a hobby but a necessity. Instead, she found something else: a garden that was not quite a vegetable garden and not quite a flower garden and not quite anything she had a name for. Plants grew in it without obvious organization. There were vegetables among the flowers and flowers among the vegetables and trees at the back that she couldn't identify and bushes at the front that were clearly doing something deliberate, though she couldn't figure out what.

···

And there was a fence. Not a tall fence, not a privacy fence, but a low fence, the kind you put up to mark a boundary and not to keep people out, a fence that said *this is mine* and also said *come in if you want to*.

···

Mrs. Okafor was waiting on the porch. She was a small woman, older than Maya had expected — seventy, maybe, or older, though she moved with the energy of someone younger. She was wearing a hat and gloves and clothes that were covered in dirt, the clothes of someone who had been working outside and had not yet changed.

···

"You must be Maya," she said. "Come in. Come in. I've made tea."

···

···

···

The tea was in the kitchen, which was also surrounded by plants — not a greenhouse, not a conservatory, just a kitchen with plants on every surface that could hold a pot, plants that were thriving despite the limited light, plants that seemed to have made their peace with the conditions and were doing what plants do when they are given even a little space and a little water and a little attention.

···

Maya took her camera out. She asked if she could photograph. Mrs. Okafor said yes, of course, which was what everyone said, though some people meant it and some people didn't, and Maya had learned to tell the difference.

···

She photographed the kitchen first. Then the garden, which was best in the morning light but which was still beautiful in the late afternoon, still full of color and texture and the particular density of a garden that has been tended for a long time. Then she photographed Mrs. Okafor, who stood still for exactly as long as she needed to and then waved her off and said *enough, enough, I'm not a model* and went back to the garden bed she had been working in when Maya arrived.

···

Maya sat on the porch and watched her work.

···

···

···

"You want to know about the garden," Mrs. Okafor said. It was not a question.

···

"I want to know about all of them," Maya said. "This one, the others. Why people stop. Why you didn't."

···

Mrs. Okafor was quiet for a moment. She was pulling weeds, the kind of weeding that does not look like weeding because she was not pulling the weeds so much as visiting them, touching each plant as she went, her hands moving over the soil with a familiarity that suggested she knew every inch of it.

···

"When I moved here," she said, "this block was full of gardens. Everyone had one. Not because we were gardeners — most of us had come from places where gardens were not optional, where the garden was the difference between eating and not eating. My mother had a garden in Nigeria. My neighbor Mrs. Patterson had a garden in Mississippi. Mr. Kimura, three doors down — he had a garden that was nothing but vegetables, the kind of garden that could feed a family."

···

She stopped. She sat back on her heels. She looked at the garden as though she were seeing it for the first time.

···

"They're all gone now. Mrs. Patterson died in 1994. Mr. Kimura moved to California to be near his children. The house where my mother lived is empty right now — no one buys these houses anymore, or the people who buy them don't want gardens, don't know what to do with all this space. They pave it over. They make it into a parking lot or a patio or something they can manage without thinking about."

···

"And you didn't," Maya said.

···

"And I didn't," Mrs. Okafor agreed. "I don't know why, exactly. I suppose because my mother planted this garden when I was a child, and when she died I couldn't bring myself to stop. It's not the same garden — I've changed it over the years, added things, taken things away. But the bones are hers. The feeling is hers. When I come out here in the morning and the light is coming through the trees and everything is growing the way it's supposed to grow, I feel her. Not her ghost — I don't believe in ghosts. But I feel the place where she used to be. The garden remembers her. That's what gardens do. They remember."

···

···

···

Maya photographed for another hour. She photographed the individual plants, the arrangements, the way the light fell through the leaves and made patterns on the soil. She photographed Mrs. Okafor's hands in the dirt, the tools leaning against the shed, the fence that marked the boundary between the garden and the street.

···

When she was done, she put the camera away and sat on the porch and drank the tea that Mrs. Okafor had made, which was hot and sweet and tasted like something she couldn't identify but that felt familiar anyway, the way certain things feel familiar even when you have never encountered them before.

···

"The project," she said. "The Last Gardens. Everyone asks me what it's about."

···

"And what do you tell them?"

···

Maya thought about this. She thought about all the neighborhoods she had visited, all the gardens she had photographed, all the people who had let her come to their yards and their houses and their memories and who had told her, in their different ways, the same thing: that the garden was not just a garden. That it was the place where something was kept alive — a memory, a connection, a way of being in the world that the world had mostly moved past.

···

"I tell them it's about what we choose to keep," she said. "And what we choose to let go. And the difference between those two choices."

···

Mrs. Okafor nodded. She was looking at the garden again, at the plants she had tended for so long, at the soil that held the remains of everything that had been planted there, including her mother's first planting and the things she had added every year since.

···

"And what do you think they'll do with it?" Mrs. Okafor asked. "When I'm gone. When the garden goes."

···

Maya didn't know. She had been asked this question before, in other neighborhoods, and she had never known what to say. The gardens she documented were, by definition, the survivors — the last of their kind, the ones that had held on when everything else had been paved over or built over or abandoned. When the people who tended them were gone, the gardens would go too. This was not pessimism. It was just the truth.

···

But there was something else, too. Something she had seen in every garden she had photographed, something that was in this garden too, in the soil and the plants and the fence that marked the boundary and the tea that tasted like something familiar.

···

"I think the gardens leave something behind," she said. "I think they change the ground. Even when the plants are gone, even when the soil is paved over — something stays. Some kind of memory in the earth."

···

Mrs. Okafor smiled. It was the smile of someone who had always known this and who was glad to hear someone else say it.

···

"That's why I keep planting," she said. "Every year. Every spring. Because the planting is the thing. Not the harvest — the planting. The putting something in the ground and believing it will grow. That's the thing worth keeping."

···

She went back to her weeding. Maya sat on the porch and watched her work.

···

The sun was going down. The light was doing what the light does at the end of the day in a garden — turning everything gold, making the ordinary look like something else, something that might not come again.

···

Maya took one more photograph. Then she put the camera away and stayed until dark. *End of story.*

*End of story.*
