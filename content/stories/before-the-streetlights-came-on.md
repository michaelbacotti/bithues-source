The streetlights in the old neighborhood came on in 1987. Delores remembers because her father kept the newspaper from the day the city council voted to approve the installation — a small clipping, folded into quarters, tucked inside the cover of his Bible. He showed it to her only once. He did not seem happy about it.

···

You will not understand, he said, what you are losing. But you will understand later, when it is gone.

···

She was eleven. She did not understand.

···

The dark had rules. Delores learned them the way children learn anything — by moving through it, by trial, by the particular teachers who lived on every block. Mrs. Bell, who sat on her porch until ten o'clock every night and sang hymns to no one in particular. The Patterson brothers, who played stickball under the single bulb that hung from the telephone pole at the corner of Maple and 5th. Old man Garrett, who walked his dog at exactly the same time every evening and knew every child's name on the street and would ask about them by name, which meant you had better have something to report.

···

The dark was not empty. The dark was full of people doing what people do when they think no one is watching — which is to say, being themselves.

···

Delores used to walk the three blocks to her friend Keisha's house after dinner, when the dark was just settling in. She was allowed to go because the dark knew her and the dark was watching and her father had made a deal with the street — he would let his children walk in it, and in exchange the street would keep them safe. This is what he believed. This is what she believed too, until the light came and the belief dissolved.

···

What she did not know then, and knows now, is that the dark was never protecting her. The dark was just the dark. What was protecting her was the fact that everyone who moved through it was also afraid — of the dark, of each other, of being seen. Fear made people careful. Care made people visible in the way that mattered, which was not the way of being seen by the light but of being seen by each other, and that was enough. That was what held.

···

Delores had a route. Every child in the neighborhood did, and every child's route was slightly different, adapted to the particular geography of what you were allowed to know and what you weren't. Her route began at her front steps, where she would stand for a moment and let the porch light behind her be the last thing, and then she would step off the concrete and into the air where the dark was thick and close and smelled of cut grass and something sweet from the dumpster behind the grocery on Burnham Avenue. She liked that smell. It meant the day was over. The day had a smell, and the night had a smell, and they were not the same.

···

She would walk east on Lynwood, past the Solis house where nothing ever happened and nothing was ever expected to happen, past the oak that had a split in its base just wide enough for her left hand if she was running. She always ran on Lynwood. It was the one block where running was allowed, not because anyone said so, but because there were no cars parked on the street and the pavement was smooth and the dark was deep enough that if you fell you would not be seen doing it. She fell sometimes. She tore her pants twice on the oak. She never told her mother.

···

At the corner of Lynwood and Burnham she would stop, because this is where the rules changed. Burnham was the main road and it ran with traffic even at night, and the light from the diner at the intersection was a yellow smear that reached far enough to make the sidewalk on the far side visible but not the one on the near side. You had to cross in the dark, which meant timing the headlights, which meant listening. Delores was good at listening. She could hear a car from two blocks away if the night was quiet, and the nights were always quiet in the part of the neighborhood that hadn't been wired yet.

···

She would cross Burnham and enter the part of the neighborhood that felt, to her, like another country. The houses here were older. The porches were deeper. The people who sat on those porches sat in folding chairs with their feet on the railing, and they talked to each other across the yards in low voices that carried in the particular way that night air carries sound — not as words exactly, but as rhythm, as music without lyrics, the rise and fall of sentences spoken by people who did not need anyone to hear them. Delores would walk past and they would sometimes fall silent, not because she was a child and children were not welcome, but because she was a person walking through the dark and the dark had its own courtesy, and the courtesy was this: let the walker pass uninterrupted. Let the walker belong to the night for as long as they were in it.

···

Keisha's house was on the corner of Maple and 5th, the last house on the block before the street bent east toward the tracks. The house had a blue door and a porch that sagged in the middle and a light inside that was always on, the one light, dim and yellow, that was part of Keisha's life even in the dark. When Delores reached the blue door she would not knock. She would stand on the sidewalk and whistle — two short, one long — and Keisha would appear in the window and then at the door, already wearing her shoes, because Keisha's shoes were never on when Delores arrived. Keisha said the walk was better with bare feet if the concrete was warm enough. It rarely was, after September. But Keisha did it anyway.

···

They would walk. This was the thing they did. Not to any particular place — the place was the walking itself, and the neighborhood was the destination, and the neighborhood at night was a story that was always being told and never quite finished. They walked south on Maple toward the tracks, past the houses with the iron fences and the houses with the chain-link and the one house, the very last one before the tracks, where an old woman they called Miss Bea kept a garden that was visible even in the dark if you knew how to look. Delores and Keisha knew how to look. The garden was a specific shape in the night, a texture of leaves and stems that caught the starlight when there was starlight and caught nothing when there was nothing, and it was the same garden every night, and every night it was different, and Miss Bea was always awake when they passed, not sitting on the porch but standing in the garden with her hands in the soil, and she would say something to them — not a greeting exactly, but a remark, a observation about the night or the season or the particular quality of the air, and then she would go back to her work and they would go on walking.

···

There was a place near the tracks where the路灯 — the old sodium lamp that the city had never gotten around to replacing — made a circle of orange light on the gravel, and they would sit there sometimes, on the concrete lip of the drainage ditch, and talk about what they talked about, which was mostly what girls talk about when no one can see their faces. Delores would say things to Keisha that she would not say to anyone, and Keisha would say things back, and the orange light was a kind of privacy, a bubble of warmth in the cold air, and the dark was a kind of permission, and the combination of the two meant that the words could be true.

···

This is what she missed most, Delores thought years later, standing at that same corner in the hard bright of the new lights. Not the dark itself — the permission. The permission to be exactly who she was in that moment, with no evidence, no record, no version of herself that could be checked against anything. The dark did not photograph you. The dark did not remember. The dark was the only place where a girl could say a thing and have it be true and then go home and be someone else and never be held to the one.

···

Keisha's mother did not like the walking. She said it was dangerous, which was the word people used when they meant something else. She meant that she did not trust the dark, which was a different thing from being afraid of it. Delores's father trusted the dark. Keisha's mother did not. These were two philosophies that coexisted in the same neighborhood without ever quite meeting, the way two streets can run parallel for blocks without touching.

···

But Keisha went anyway. She was like that — gentle in appearance but stubborn underneath, the kind of stubborn that waits until no one is watching and then quietly does the thing that was forbidden. She and Delores never discussed Keisha's mother's rules. They simply did not apply to the dark, because the dark did not recognize them. The dark had its own governance, and it was looser and stranger and, they both believed, more fair.

···

They talked about leaving, sometimes. Not the neighborhood — they could not imagine leaving the neighborhood — but the circumstances. They talked about the lives they would have when they were old enough to choose, and those lives were always the same life, shared, with a house somewhere far from here and a garden and a porch and no lights at all on the street outside. They would sit on that porch — they could see it perfectly, even then — and they would talk the way they were talking now, in the orange light, in the cold air, with no one watching and nothing to prove. Keisha would have a dog. Delores would learn to cook something real. The dark would come every night and it would be the same dark they knew, the dark that was theirs.

···

They were eleven. They did not know yet that the place you imagine is not the place you get, and that the future is not a destination but a series of detours that look, from the inside, like the only possible road.

···

The first streetlight was installed on the corner of Elm and 4th, outside Mrs. Carter's house. It took four months for the rest to follow. Delores watched them come one by one, like something being stitched into the sky, and each time she felt the neighborhood contract — the porch lights go out earlier, the voices stop sooner, the rhythm of the evening shift toward something she did not recognize.

···

Keisha noticed it too. They used to meet at the corner of Maple and 5th, at the telephone pole with the single bulb, and talk for an hour before going home. After the lights came, they stopped meeting. Not because they decided to stop — because the place where they used to meet stopped existing. The corner was still there. The pole was still there. But the thing that had made it a place — the dark that held it — was gone.

···

You could see everything now. That was the problem. You could see, which meant everyone else could see you, which meant no one was being themselves anymore. They were being seen. There is a difference. Delores felt it that first winter, when she walked to Keisha's house and found her sitting on the porch with the television on and the door open and a look on her face that said she had forgotten what the porch was for.

···

The dark had a purpose. The light just showed you what you'd already decided was there.

···

Keisha did not talk the way she used to talk after the lights came. Not because she had changed — she was still Keisha, still stubborn and soft-footed and brave in the specific way that meant doing the thing that was forbidden when no one was watching — but because there was no place left where talking felt safe. The new lights made the porches visible from the street, and the streets visible from the porches, and what had been private was now a matter of geography, and the geography had changed, and Keisha was a different person in the new geography. Delores understood this. She was a different person too. They were still friends — they would be friends for another six years, through high school, through the years when Keisha's mother finally succeeded in moving them to a town two hours north where the streetlights had been there from the beginning — but they were not the people they had been in the dark, and neither of them knew how to talk about that.

···

Keisha left in March of 1993. She did not say goodbye in the usual way. She called at seven in the evening, which was the old time, the time when they would have been walking, and said: my mother says we are moving. I wanted to tell you first. Delores stood in her own hallway with the phone cord wrapped around her fingers and listened to the voice that she had heard in the orange light say the thing that was true — I will miss the walking — and she said it back, and they did not cry, because crying was something you did when you were alone, and they were not alone yet, not quite, not until the line went quiet.

···

Delores is fifty-three now. She lives in a different neighborhood, one that was built after the streetlight era and carries none of its history. She thinks about the old neighborhood sometimes — not with nostalgia, exactly, but with the feeling you get when you realize you've lost something and can't remember what it was.

···

The other day she drove back through it. The houses are smaller than she remembered. The trees are gone. The corner of Maple and 5th has a new pole, new lights, a sign advertising a laundromat that wasn't there before. Everything is visible. Nothing is hidden. She sat at the corner for ten minutes and waited to feel what she used to feel there, but the corner didn't know her anymore, and the dark was gone, and the thing she had lost had a name after all, even if no one had ever written it down.

···

It was called being unknown. It was called belonging to the night. It was called the hours before the light arrived, when anyone could be anyone, and no one was watching, and the world was still deciding what it wanted to be.

···

She drove home in the dark, which is not the same thing as what she remembered, but is close enough to make her sad.

···

She has thought, sometimes, about writing to Keisha. The last she heard — this was years ago, through the old channels, the grapevine that runs through every neighborhood whether it has streetlights or not — Keisha was living in the same town her mother had chosen, working at a school, raising two daughters. Delores has imagined the conversation they might have, the two of them at sixty or seventy, sitting on some porch in some place that is finally quiet, finally dark enough, saying the things they never said when they were eleven and had a garden near the tracks and a circle of orange light and the whole night ahead of them and no idea that they were living in the part of their lives they would spend the rest of their lives missing.

···

She has not written. Some things, she thinks, belong to the dark. You cannot bring them into the light without changing what they are. You cannot translate them into language without losing whatever it was that made them true in the first place. Keisha would understand this. Keisha, who walked barefoot on cold concrete because the walking was better with bare feet, and who believed in the dark even after the dark was gone, and who said on the phone that last evening: I will miss the walking. And meant it. And did not know yet that missing the walking was not a feeling you recovered from, but a condition you learned to live inside, the way some people learn to live in a house where someone they loved once lived. You do not get over it. You furnish around it. You build a life in the rooms that are still yours, and you leave the dark room exactly as it was, and you do not turn on the light when you pass the door.

···

Delores still walks at night. Not in her neighborhood — the streets are too bright, the traffic too present, the world too awake in the way that cities are awake now, everywhere, all the time. She drives to a place on the outskirts, a road that runs along the edge of a field with no houses on it, and she parks with the headlights off and sits in the car with the windows down, and she waits for the dark to arrive the way it used to arrive, slow and certain, filling the air from the ground up. It is not the same dark. It is not even close. But it is dark enough to remind her that she was once a girl who walked through a neighborhood that belonged to her, and she knew every corner of it, and she knew who she was in the dark between the porch lights, and she has never since been as certain of anything as she was of that.
