The woman who worked as a listener had a name but used it rarely. Her patients called her the Listener, which was not a title she had chosen but which she had come to accept, because it was accurate, because it was the thing she did, because after twenty years the title had become her the way titles sometimes do when they fit closely enough to be indistinguishable from the person wearing them.

···

She sat with the dying. This was not a clinical role — she was not a nurse, not a doctor, not a social worker. She had no medical training. She had no therapeutic credential. What she had was something simpler and harder to describe: she was willing to be present. She was willing to be in a room with someone who was leaving and to stay there, without agenda, without direction, without the particular agenda that visitors bring, which is to say the need to be comforted, to be helped, to be given something that would make the dying person's situation better than it was.

···

She did not help. She received.

···

···

···

Today she was assigned to a patient she knew. This happened sometimes — not often, but enough that she had developed a practice for it. Today the patient was named Ruth, and Ruth was seventy-nine years old, and she was dying of the same thing most people Ruth's age were dying of, which was time, which was the body running down in the way that bodies do when they have done the work of living and have nothing left to give.

···

Ruth had been her friend.

···

This was the complication. This was the thing that made the assignment different from all the others, because the others had been strangers, and Ruth was not a stranger, and the Listener had to sit with Ruth's dying knowing what she knew about the person she was sitting with, which was everything, which was thirty years of conversation and disagreement and the particular closeness that comes from people who have known each other long enough to have seen each other at their worst and stayed anyway.

···

···

···

Ruth was in a room on the fourth floor. The Listener came in and closed the door behind her and sat in the chair beside the bed. Ruth's eyes were closed but she was not asleep — the Listener knew the difference, had learned it over twenty years, the particular quality of the breathing that meant someone was awake but resting, waiting but not hoping.

···

"I know you're here," Ruth said.

···

"I know you know."

···

The Listener had brought nothing with her. She never brought anything. She sat in the chair and she waited and she let Ruth speak when Ruth was ready, or let the silence be if Ruth was not ready to speak.

···

Ruth was not ready to speak. She was quiet for a long time. The Listener sat.

···

···

···

They had met at a conference in 1996. The Listener had been twenty-eight and working on her first book, a study of end-of-life care practices in different cultures. Ruth had been a palliative care nurse who had been asked to speak on a panel about what patients actually wanted in their final days. The Listener had gone to every session Ruth spoke at. They had had dinner afterward, and dinner had turned into dinner the next night, and dinner the next night, and a friendship that neither of them had expected and both of them had kept.

···

Ruth had taught the Listener how to do what she did. Not formally — Ruth had never been her supervisor, never been her mentor in any official sense. But Ruth had shown her, through the way she moved through a room, the way she sat with a patient, the way she listened — had shown her what it meant to receive another person's dying without trying to change it, without trying to make it easier, without trying to do anything except be there.

···

"You listen like a nurse," someone had said to the Listener once.

···

"That's the compliment I want," the Listener had said.

···

···

···

"Will you tell me something?" Ruth said.

···

"Anything."

···

"I don't know what I want you to tell me. That's the problem. I know what I'm supposed to want — I know what the books say people want at the end. They want to resolve things. They want to say goodbye. They want to make peace with whatever they've done wrong. They want to hear that they're forgiven."

···

"Those things are not available to me. I can only tell you what I have."

···

"What do you have?"

···

The Listener sat with the question. This was the practice — to sit with the question, even when it was Ruth's question, even when she knew Ruth well enough to know what kind of answer would be comfortable and what kind would not.

···

"I have the truth," the Listener said. "I have what I observed. I have twenty years of watching you move through the world and what I saw in you, which was someone who was trying, always, to be of use. Not to be comfortable. Not to be happy. To be useful. You were useful to me, Ruth. You showed me how to do this work. You showed me what it meant to receive someone without trying to fix them."

···

Ruth's eyes were still closed. The Listener could see her breathing, the slow rhythm of it, the way it had slowed in the last weeks in a way that was not alarming but was noticeable, was the body's way of saying what it was preparing for.

···

"That's not forgiveness," Ruth said.

···

"No. It's not."

···

"I don't want to be forgiven. I want to be understood. I want someone to understand what it was like. Not to apologize for it. Not to explain it. Just to understand it."

···

The Listener understood. She had been doing this for twenty years. She knew what understanding meant in this context — not agreement, not absolution, not the removal of judgment. Understanding was simply the willingness to hold what someone had done or what had been done to them without collapsing it into something simpler than it was.

···

"I'm here," the Listener said. "I'm listening. Tell me what it was like."

···

···

···

Ruth talked for two hours. She talked about her childhood, which had been hard in a way that was not interesting to anyone except her and was now, at the end, interesting only to her and to whoever was willing to hear it. She talked about her marriage, which had been good and then bad and then something else that she had never found a name for. She talked about the patients she had sat with, the hundreds of them, the thousands of them, the ones she remembered and the ones she had let go.

···

The Listener listened. She did not interrupt. She did not ask questions. She did not offer comfort or interpretation or the particular response that people offer when they are listening and are afraid that the silence means they are not doing their job.

···

The silence was the job.

···

···

···

At the end, Ruth was quiet. The Listener held her hand. The hand was thin — bone and skin and the particular weight of someone who was leaving.

···

"I'm glad it's you," Ruth said.

···

"I know."

···

"You won't forget this."

···

"I won't."

···

"I want you to know that I'm not afraid. I thought I would be afraid. I'm not. I'm just — tired. And ready. And I wanted you to be here because you understand what it means. You've been here before. You know what I'm doing."

···

The Listener knew. She had been here before — not with Ruth, but with others. The moment when the dying person let go of the need to explain, to justify, to make sense of what was happening. The moment when they simply rested in what was, which was the last thing any of us get to do.

···

"I'm here," the Listener said.

···

She held Ruth's hand. She stayed.

···

The afternoon light moved across the floor. The machines beeped their small, precise rhythms. The Listener sat, and received, and let Ruth be what Ruth was, which was a woman in the last moments of a life that had been complicated and ordinary and entirely her own.

···

Ruth died at 4:47 in the afternoon. The Listener was still there.

···

She stayed for another hour. She did not call the nurses yet. She sat in the room with the body that had been Ruth, and she let the silence be what it was, and she thought about twenty years of work, and what it meant, and what it would mean when she could no longer do it.

···

Then she stood up. She straightened her jacket. She walked out of the room and down the hall and out into the world, where there were other people who were dying and who needed someone to listen, and who would wait, as they always waited, for her to arrive.

···

She went to the next assignment.

···

She always did. *End of story.*

*End of story.*
