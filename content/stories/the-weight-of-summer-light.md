The house was smaller than she remembered. This was the first thing Clara noticed, standing on the sidewalk in the August heat, looking at the place where she had grown up. The house she'd left at eighteen had seemed enormous — a world unto itself, with its creaking stairs and slanted floors and the particular way each room held a different quality of light. Now it was just a house. A small house, by current standards, with aluminum siding that had not been there when she was a child and a porch that had clearly not been painted in years.

···

Her mother had died in March. The house had been empty since then, and Clara had come to clean it, to prepare it for sale, to do the things that daughters do when their mothers die and leave behind the spaces they once filled. She had not expected the August heat. She had not expected the way the light would fall through the windows in exactly the same angles it had fallen when she was nine, when she was fourteen, when she was seventeen and angry about something and hiding in her room with a book.

···

She went inside.

···

···

···

The smell was still there. This surprised her — she had assumed that three months of emptiness would have dispelled whatever it was that made this house smell like itself, the particular combination of old wood and carpet cleaner and the lavender sachets her mother had kept in every drawer. But the smell was there, unchanged, and Clara stood in the hallway and breathed it and felt something shift in her chest that she could not name.

···

The hallway was narrow. The floor was hardwood, visible where the carpet had been removed — the carpet her mother had put in in 1987, the year Clara turned twelve and had opinions about floors that she had somehow been allowed to express. She remembered the argument about the carpet. She remembered her mother saying *this is what we're doing* and Clara saying *but I don't like it* and her mother saying *you don't have to like it, you have to live with it*, which was a phrase her mother used often and which Clara had never understood until she was older and realized that most of life was things you didn't like but had to live with.

···

The floor was hardwood now. The carpet was gone. Clara walked down the hall and felt the boards creak under her feet in the exact places they had always creaked, and she thought about how some things don't change even when everything else does, and how the body remembers what the mind lets go.

···

···

···

The kitchen was at the back of the house, as kitchens had been in the 1960s houses of that era — not the center of family life but the辅助 space, the place you went to cook and eat and have conversations that weren't quite important enough for the living room. Clara stood in the doorway and looked at the kitchen table.

···

The table was still there. The same table that had been there since she was five years old, the table where she had done homework and eaten cereal and had conversations with her mother about things that seemed important at the time and that she could not now remember. The table had scratches in it — marks from when she had tried to carve her name into the wood with a butter knife and had been caught and punished and made to sand the scratches out, which had taken an entire Saturday and which she had resented at the time and which she now found almost unbearably sweet.

···

Her mother had kept the table. She had kept all of it — the chairs with the uneven legs, the refrigerator with the dent in the side that came from the time Clara backed into it while learning to drive, the window that looked out on the backyard and the garden that was now just grass, gone wild, gone to seed.

···

Clara opened the refrigerator. It was empty except for a box of baking soda and a bottle of hot sauce that she recognized as her mother's — the same hot sauce her mother had used on everything, the hot sauce that Clara had never been able to stand and that she had eventually come to associate with the taste of her mother's cooking, the taste of home, the taste of a kitchen that was still, even empty, still full of something.

···

···

···

She cleaned for three days. She emptied cabinets and scrubbed surfaces and threw away things that had accumulated in the way that things accumulate in houses where people live for decades and never quite manage to sort through everything. She found her old report cards in a drawer in the bedroom. She found letters she had written to her mother from college, letters her mother had saved and organized and kept in a shoebox that was labeled, in her mother's handwriting, *Clara — college years*. She found photographs she had never seen — her mother as a young woman, before Clara was born, before the life that Clara had known had started.

···

She sat on her mother's bed and looked at the photographs for a long time.

···

···

···

The last night she stayed in the house was a Saturday. She had finished the cleaning. The house was ready to be sold. She had done everything she came to do, and she had nowhere else to be, and she stayed anyway — not because she needed to but because leaving on the last night felt like abandoning something that deserved a longer goodbye.

···

She sat on the back porch. The porch was wooden, unsupported, the kind of porch that had been added to the house at some point in its history by someone who had thought a porch would be nice and who had not thought about how the wood would rot and how the supports would weaken and how the porch would eventually become a place you couldn't really use because it felt like it might give way under your weight.

···

She sat on the porch anyway. She had always been drawn to the precarious things.

···

The light was changing. It was late afternoon, the time when the sun moved toward the horizon and the light became something else — became the particular quality of August light that Clara had always thought of as *the weight of summer light*, the light that fell on everything and made everything look the way it was supposed to look, heavy and golden and final.

···

Her mother had loved this time of day. She had stood in the kitchen at this hour and looked out the window and said something about how the garden looked at this hour, how the light was best at this hour, how this hour was her favorite and how Clara should pay attention to these things because these were the things that mattered, the small observations, the daily noting of what the light was doing and what it was leaving behind.

···

Clara had not paid attention. She had been seventeen and certain that her mother's observations were the small obsessions of a small mind, that the things that mattered were larger than this, that she needed to get out of this house and into a life that was bigger and more important than anything she could see from this porch.

···

She had gotten out. She had gotten into the larger life. She had done things and seen things and become someone that her teenage self would have recognized as successful, as important, as someone who had made something of herself.

···

And now her mother was dead, and the house was empty, and she was sitting on a porch that might not hold her weight, and the light was doing what it had always done in August, which was to make everything look the way it was supposed to look, which was to say: this is what summer looks like when it's ending. This is what you get to keep.

···

···

···

She stayed until dark. She watched the light fade. She listened to the sounds of the neighborhood — the distant traffic, the dog that barked every evening at the same time, the particular silence of a Saturday night in a town where most people were inside doing the things that people do on Saturday nights.

···

She thought about going back to her hotel. She thought about the drive back to her life, which was waiting for her, which had been waiting for her since she arrived, which was the life she had chosen and that she mostly liked and that was, by every measure she had been taught to use, good.

···

She thought about the house. She thought about her mother. She thought about the things she had not said and the things she had said wrong and the things she had said too late, and she thought about how this was what grief was — not the absence of someone, but the presence of everything you didn't say.

···

She stood up. The porch creaked. She went inside.

···

···

···

In the morning, she locked the door. She put the key in the lockbox. She stood on the sidewalk and looked at the house one more time.

···

It was still small. It was still the house she had grown up in. It was still the place where her mother had lived and died and kept the lavender sachets in every drawer and watched the light change at the end of August.

···

Clara got in her car. She drove to the airport. She flew home.

···

The house waited. It would be sold. New people would come. They would paint the walls and replace the floors and make the changes that new owners make, and the house would become theirs, would become a different house, would stop being the house it had been.

···

But the light would still come through the windows in August. The boards would still creak in the hallway. The smell would still be there, at least for a while, at least until the house forgot what it had held.

···

And Clara would remember. She would remember the weight of summer light and the sound of her mother's voice saying *pay attention to these things* and the particular way the world looked on a Saturday afternoon in August when you were standing on a porch that might not hold you and you were not afraid, because the thing you were afraid of had already happened, and you were still here, and the light was still doing what it had always done, which was to make everything look the way it was supposed to look.

···

She let it. *End of story.*

*End of story.*
