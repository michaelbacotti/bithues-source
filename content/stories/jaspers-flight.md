The dreams began the summer Jasper turned seven. That was also the summer his mother stopped painting and started sleeping until noon, so neither of them talked about it much at first. His father had been gone for seven months by then — not dead, just absent in the way that leaves more questions than silence does — and the apartment had settled into a new quiet, the kind that presses against the walls.

···

In the first dream, Jasper stood on the roof of their building and the city stretched out below him like a circuit board, all amber lights and dark arteries of streets. He could feel the wind threading through his fingers, and when he leaned forward and let himself fall, he didn't fall. He floated outward, over the rooftops, over the bridge where the river caught the moon, over everything he knew. He woke with his heart slamming against his ribs and his hands tingling as if he'd held them too close to a fire.

···

He didn't tell anyone. Seven-year-olds know things about what adults will believe, and Jasper had already learned that some truths are wasted on people who aren't ready for them.

···

The dreams continued. Each night — or almost each night — he would climb the imaginary stairs to the roof and let himself fall into the dark. The falling was always the same: that moment of surrender when your body says *no* and some other part of you says *yes, lean, open, let go.* And then the air would catch him like a hand catching a breath, and he would drift.

···

What he felt in those moments was something he had no word for. Not joy exactly. Not freedom. Something more like recognition — as if he'd been waiting his whole short life for his body to remember what his mind had never been taught.

···

His mother noticed he was tired. She blamed the nightmares she assumed he was having, the ones children have about monsters and falling. She started leaving the hallway light on, which made it worse, because the glow cut the darkness into wrong shapes.

···

"It's okay," she said one morning, finding him at the kitchen table with his cornflakes going soft. "Everybody has scary dreams. They're not real."

···

Jasper looked at her. Her hair was unwashed, pulled back with a clip he didn't recognize. She was wearing the old grey T-shirt she slept in. She had been beautiful once — he'd seen the photographs — and she still was, in a way that required him to look past the exhaustion to find it.

···

"They're real," he said, and then, because he didn't know how to explain it: "They're the most real things there are."

···

She smiled the way adults do when children say things that are too big for them. "Eat your cereal, sweetheart."

···

···

···

The summer ended. School started. Jasper kept the dreams to himself and learned to function on less sleep than a ten-year-old should need. He did his homework. He ate dinner. He watched television with his mother in the evenings when she was awake, and when she wasn't, he read books from the library — adventure stories, mostly, with characters who sailed across oceans and climbed mountains and never once felt the particular terror of standing on a ledge and wanting to jump.

···

He started paying attention to his body during the day. He noticed that when he climbed the jungle gym at recess, his hands knew something his feet didn't. When he ran, he sometimes felt a ghost-pressure against his back, as if the air were nudging him forward faster than his legs could carry him. He started jumping off low walls, not to hurt himself but to test something, to see if the fall would turn into something else.

···

It never did. Not during the day. Only at night, in the space between sleeping and waking, when the rules of the world seemed to pause and take a breath.

···

He was eleven when he finally decided to tell someone. Not his mother — she'd gotten worse that year, retreating into a silence that felt like weather, something that had moved in and made itself at home. He told Mrs. Alvarez, the school counselor, because she had kind eyes and because she'd asked him specifically how he was sleeping.

···

"I can fly," he told her. "In my dreams. I can fly."

···

Mrs. Alvarez nodded slowly, the way people do when they're being very careful. "That sounds like a wonderful dream, Jasper. A lot of children wish they could fly."

···

"It's not a wish," he said. "It's not a dream. It's — " He searched for the word. "It's remembering."

···

She referred him to a child psychologist. The psychologist asked him to draw pictures of his dreams and told his mother that Jasper had a "lively imagination" and was "processing some complex emotions about his father's absence." He was prescribed a sleep study that his insurance wouldn't cover, and the subject was quietly dropped.

···

Jasper stopped talking about it after that. He was twelve, and he'd learned enough about the world to know that some things, no matter how true they feel, will always sound like lies.

···

···

···

He was fourteen when the thing happened on the roof of the Miller Building.

···

He'd climbed up there with two kids from school — Kevin Marsh and a boy named Diego whose family had just moved from Guatemala — because they'd heard there were pigeons and because they were fourteen and old enough to know better and not old enough to care. It was November, cold enough that their breath fogged in the air, and they stood at the edge of the roof looking down at the street eight stories below.

···

Kevin made a joke about jumping. Everyone laughed. Then Diego said, very quietly, "I couldn't do it anyway. I'm scared of heights."

···

"Everyone's scared of heights," Kevin said. "It's normal."

···

"No," Diego said. "I'm scared of falling. Not of being up here. There's a difference."

···

Jasper looked at the edge, at the dark air between where he stood and where the sidewalk waited. He felt the old familiar pull — the same sensation from his dreams, the one that said *lean, open, let go.* But this time he didn't want to fly. This time he wanted to test something.

···

He stepped off the roof.

···

Not all the way. He stepped past the safety ledge, the low concrete barrier that separated them from the edge, and he put one foot out into the empty air and felt it.

···

Felt nothing.

···

His foot hung over the void. His body remained perfectly still. No invisible hand caught him. No wind lifted him. He was just a boy standing on a roof with one foot in the wrong place, and the air was cold and ordinary and did not care about him at all.

···

He stepped back. Kevin was staring at him. Diego looked like he was going to be sick.

···

"What the hell," Kevin said.

···

Jasper didn't answer. He went home and lay in bed and waited for darkness and the old familiar surrender.

···

That night he dreamed, and he flew, and he understood nothing.

···

···

···

The years passed the way years do when you're young and things are wrong — too fast and too slow at the same time, each day stretching to hold the weight of everything you can't say. Jasper graduated middle school, then high school. His mother got better slowly, the way people do when the darkness decides to move on to someone else. His father sent a card on his birthday, then stopped.

···

Jasper was seventeen the first time he flew while awake.

···

He was in his bedroom. It was March, three in the morning, and he couldn't sleep — the old insomnia had settled back in, that particular companion that never really leaves once it's found you. He was lying on his back on the floor because he'd read somewhere that it helps, that the hardness of the ground can ground you, and he was thinking about nothing in particular when his body simply rose.

···

Not a dream. Not a vision. His body lifted off the floor and drifted upward until his head touched the ceiling, and then he hung there, parallel to the ground, perfectly still, his arms at his sides and his eyes open and the streetlight outside his window painting yellow stripes across the ceiling.

···

He did not panic. That was the strangest part. He felt the fear he should have felt — the ancient animal terror of falling, of being unsupported, of the ground that should be there and wasn't — but underneath it was something else. Calm. Recognition. The feeling of something clicking into place after being misaligned for years.

···

He floated to the floor and landed on his feet, and the room looked exactly the same as it had before, and his body felt exactly the same, and he understood that this was the moment everything was going to be different.

···

He did not tell anyone. Who would he tell? What would he say? *I can fly. Not in my dreams. In my life.* He could already hear the words people would use: psychotic break, hallucination, delusion. He was seventeen, he was apparently mentally ill, and the world did not have a category for what he was.

···

So he kept it to himself. He learned to control it — learned that it came when he stopped fighting, when he let the fear flow through him and out the other side. He learned to rise and fall, to drift across his room, to hover by the window and watch the street below like a bird watching worms.

···

He did not go outside. He was not stupid. He was not ready to become a headline.

···

···

···

The summer he turned eighteen, his mother found out.

···

She came home early from a date — she'd started dating again, a man named Paul who sold furniture and laughed too loudly — and she came upstairs to tell him something about dinner and she found him.

···

He was in the kitchen. He'd been practicing, trying to build up the muscle of it, the will of it, and he hadn't heard her key in the lock. He was two feet off the ground, hovering near the ceiling with a glass of water in his hand, and when she walked in he was simply there, hanging in the air like something that had always belonged there.

···

The glass fell. It shattered against the floor. Neither of them moved for a long moment.

···

"Jasper," she said. Her voice was very quiet. "What are you doing."

···

He could have lied. He could have said he'd been jumping, that he'd somehow gotten caught on something, that it was a trick of the light. He was eighteen and he was tired of lies, tired of the separate architecture of his life, tired of being the only one who knew the true shape of things.

···

"I'm flying," he said.

···

"That's not possible."

···

"I know," he said. "I know it isn't."

···

He descended slowly, the way he'd learned to do — not falling but settling, like a leaf or a snowflake, something that had always known how to reach the ground without fear. His bare feet touched the tile. The shattered glass crunched beneath them.

···

She was staring at him like he'd become something else, something her eyes couldn't reconcile with the person she thought she knew. And maybe he was. Maybe he had been becoming this all along, and the dreams had been the slow work of it, the long preparation for a life that didn't obey the usual rules.

···

"How long," she said.

···

"Since I was seven."

···

Seventeen years of secrets, and he said it in four words, and she said nothing, and the kitchen filled with the weight of everything that had gone unsaid.

···

She sat down on one of the kitchen chairs. She put her face in her hands. He stood in the broken glass and waited, because he didn't know what else to do.

···

"I thought it was my fault," she said, eventually, muffled by her hands. "I thought I did something wrong, when you were little. I thought — you were always somewhere else. Even when you were right in front of me. You were always somewhere far away."

···

"I was flying," he said. "In my head. I was flying."

···

She looked up at him, and her eyes were red, and her face was the same face it had always been, and he understood that this was the truest thing they'd ever shared with each other.

···

···

···

He left home at nineteen. Not because of the flying — because of the usual things, the ordinary forces that push young men out into the world. College applications and financial aid and the desire to be somewhere that didn't contain his childhood bedroom and all its ghosts.

···

He went to a school four hundred miles away, far enough that the familiar sky didn't look down on him, and he lived in a dorm room and studied architecture and told no one about what he could do. He was good at pretending. He'd had years of practice.

···

But at night, when the campus was quiet, he would rise from his bed and drift to the window and open it and slip out into the air. He learned the city from above — the angles of rooftops, the cathedral spires, the parks that held their darkness like cupped water. He learned how to move without sound, how to let the wind carry him, how to fold himself into updrafts and ride them like a raptor.

···

He never flew high. He never flew far. He was still afraid, still that small boy standing at the edge of a roof, still waiting for the world to prove him wrong.

···

But he flew.

···

···

···

He was twenty-three when he finally flew in front of someone else on purpose.

···

Her name was Linh. She was a graduate student in the physics department who worked nights at the campus library, and he had been finding excuses to be there for weeks — not because he wanted to talk to her but because he wanted to be near someone who didn't know. She was quiet and serious and she wore glasses that she was always pushing up her nose, and when she smiled it was like watching a door open in a wall that had been solid for years.

···

One night in December, he came in late and she was the only one there, and she looked up from her books and said, "You come here a lot."

···

"I like the quiet."

···

"The library is closing in twenty minutes."

···

"I know."

···

She closed her book. She looked at him the way she looked at everything — carefully, like she was cataloging details for later. "What's your name?"

···

"Jasper."

···

"I'm Linh."

···

"I know."

···

She smiled. "That's not creepy at all."

···

"I'm not — I'm not trying to be creepy. I just think you're interesting. And I don't say that to very many people."

···

She studied him for a moment, and then she said, "Do you want to get coffee? There's an all-night place on Maple Street."

···

They walked to the coffee shop. They talked about nothing — her research, his classes, the winter that was coming on too fast. At two in the morning they walked back across the empty campus, and he was telling her something about his father's absence, a story he'd never told anyone, and the words came out and kept coming out, and she listened, and somewhere in the middle of it she said, "You look like you're floating."

···

He stopped walking. "What?"

···

"You walk like you're not quite touching the ground. It's — I don't know. It's interesting. You look like you're lighter than you should be."

···

He looked down at his feet. They were touching the pavement. They had always been touching the pavement. But she had seen something, some quality of movement that his body had learned and his mind had forgotten, the way a person who's been swimming for years still moves differently on land, carrying the memory of the water in their muscles.

···

"Linh," he said. "I need to show you something."

···

Not because he loved her — he barely knew her. Not because he trusted her — he'd known her for weeks. But because he was twenty-three and he was tired of the separate life, the double architecture, the self that was real and the self that performed, and she was the first person who had looked at him and seen something true even when she couldn't have known what it was.

···

He stepped into the empty lawn beside the science building and he rose. He rose the way he always did — not dramatically, not with a flourish, but slowly, like a diver ascending from the deep, and he kept rising until he was ten feet off the ground, fifteen, twenty, until the streetlights were below him and the sky was close and his body was horizontal, parallel to the earth, his arms at his sides and his face toward the stars.

···

Linh stood on the grass and looked up at him. She did not scream. She did not run. She put her hands in her coat pockets and she watched, and when he finally descended — slowly, carefully, giving her time to understand that he was still the same person she had been talking to moments before — she said:

···

"Well. That explains the walking."

···

He laughed. He laughed for the first time in months. She smiled at him, that smile that was like a door opening, and she said: "Does it work in coffee shops? Do you have to check your physics at the door?"

···

"I don't know," he said. "I've never tried."

···

"Try it," she said. "I'm writing my thesis on theoretical dimensions. I could use the data."

···

He flew in coffee shops after that. Not all the time, not obviously — but sometimes, late at night, when the places were almost empty and the ceiling was low enough that it felt safe, he would rise up out of his chair and drift toward the ceiling and back down again, and she would take notes and ask questions and treat him like a phenomenon rather than a freak, which was exactly what he had needed.

···

···

···

He was thirty-one when his mother died. Cancer, the kind that moves fast, the kind that doesn't give you time to prepare your grief. He flew home for the funeral, and he flew back to the apartment afterward, and he stood on the roof of the Miller Building — the same roof where he had stepped off at fourteen and learned nothing — and he rose.

···

He rose higher than he had ever risen. Above the city, above the light pollution, above the clouds. The world fell away beneath him — the streets and the buildings and the rivers and the farms — until he was alone in the dark with the stars, and he hung there in the silence and let himself feel everything he had been too afraid to feel while she was alive.

···

He stayed up there for an hour. Maybe more. The cold didn't touch him. The altitude didn't matter. He was flying, the way he had always flown, and for the first time he understood that it had never been about the dreams. The dreams had been the invitation. The flying was the answer. The world had been telling him, all along, what he was for.

···

He came down slowly. He walked home through the empty streets. The sun was coming up by the time he reached his apartment, and he lay down on his bed, and he slept without dreaming.

···

When he woke, he knew what he had to do.

···

···

···

He started small. A video, posted anonymously, grainy and unclear — a figure rising from a rooftop in a city that could have been any city, holding still in the air for a moment before drifting higher and out of frame. It accumulated a few million views. Commenters called it CGI, hoax, edited footage. The news called it a "viral mystery."

···

He didn't care. He uploaded another. And another. He stopped trying to hide his face. He let them see him — a man in a grey hoodie, rising from parking lots and bridges and empty fields, always at night, always alone. He was careful not to fly above the clouds. He was careful not to go fast. He was careful not to give them anything they could shoot at.

···

The world didn't believe him. That was the interesting part. The world had built itself on the assumption that people cannot fly, and it refused — politely, firmly — to update its assumptions. The videos were declared fake. The comments sections filled with explanations. A physicist wrote an op-ed about "pareidolia for the airborne." A comedian made a joke on late-night television. Jasper watched it from his apartment, eating cereal, and felt nothing but a strange and tender pity.

···

They would believe him eventually. Or they wouldn't. It didn't matter. What mattered was the flying itself — the act of it, the doing of it — and the knowledge that he was no longer alone with what he was.

···

He started teaching, in his way. Small things. He would land in a public park and let someone see him do it — a jogger at dawn, a teenager sneaking out late, an old woman sitting on her porch at three in the morning because she couldn't sleep — and he would talk to them, and they would stare, and sometimes they would ask questions, and he would answer honestly.

···

*How do you do it?*

···

You stop being afraid of falling.

···

*That's it?*

···

That's it. And you remember that you were always meant for the air. You were just told to forget.

···

They would nod, or shake their heads, or start crying, or walk away. It didn't matter. He was planting seeds. Some of them would grow.

···

···

···

He was forty-five when the boy found him.

···

The boy was twelve. He had dark hair and hollow cheeks and he was sitting on the roof of a building in a city Jasper didn't know, and when Jasper drifted up beside him and hovered at the roof's edge, the boy looked at him without fear.

···

"I had a dream like that," the boy said. "For years. I could fly, and then I woke up, and I couldn't."

···

Jasper settled onto the roof. He sat beside the boy, their legs hanging over the edge, the city spread out below them like a circuit board, all amber lights and dark arteries of streets.

···

"It's not a dream," Jasper said. "It's a door."

···

The boy looked at him. His eyes were old in a way that made Jasper's chest ache.

···

"How do you open it?"

···

"You already did," Jasper said. "You just have to walk through."

···

The boy was quiet for a long time. The wind moved between them, carrying the sound of traffic and voices and a world that didn't know what it was on the verge of understanding.

···

"Will you show me?" the boy said.

···

Jasper stood. He held out his hand. The boy looked at it, and then he took it, and Jasper pulled him up, and he rose — slowly, gently, holding the boy's hand and rising with him until they were both floating, side by side, ten feet above the rooftop, twenty, thirty, until the city was a map below them and the sky was close and open and endless.

···

The boy didn't scream. He didn't cry. He looked down at his feet, which were not touching anything, and he looked at Jasper, and he said:

···

"Oh."

···

"Yeah," Jasper said. "Oh."

···

They flew. They flew until the sun came up, and then they flew some more, and when they finally landed, the boy was crying, and Jasper let him, because he remembered what it felt like to finally come home.
