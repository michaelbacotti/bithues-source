The machine was not what anyone would have imagined. There were no sparks, no dramatic lighting, no thunderous sound effects. It was a box — matte black, roughly the size of a shipping container, with a single aperture on one side that was exactly the size and shape of a human eye. The aperture was covered by a lens that Dr. Yuki Tanaka had spent seven years perfecting, a lens that could resolve images at a resolution that should not have been possible with any known technology.

···

The rest of the equipment was equally mundane: computers, monitors, cabling, the detritus of a research program that had consumed most of a career and all of a budget. The machine sat in a basement at the university, humming quietly, waiting for the moment when someone would be brave enough or foolish enough to look through the door it had opened.

···

Yuki was both.

···

···

···

The theory had been her own, developed in the early years of her career when she was still an assistant professor with more ambition than tenure and a willingness to pursue questions that her colleagues considered fringe. She had published the initial papers in a journal that most physicists never read, and she had spent the next decade building toward the machine that would prove her right.

···

The theory was simple, in the way that the most important theories are simple. The universe was not singular. There were other universes — infinitely many of them — that existed alongside this one, separated by dimensions that the human mind was not equipped to perceive. These other universes were not distant in space; they were distant in configuration, in the particular arrangement of matter and energy that defined them. They were here, all around us, occupying the same space we occupied, layered like pages in a book that could not be opened because there was no seam to cut.

···

The machine could see through that seam. It could look at the other pages.

···

···

···

She had tested it first with cameras, then with instruments, then with animals. The results were always the same: on the other side of the aperture, there was another world. It looked like this world — the same laboratory, the same equipment, the same physical laws — but different in ways that the instruments could measure and the animals could sense. The animals, when placed in front of the aperture, showed elevated stress hormones. They knew something was there. They didn't know what.

···

Yuki had resisted the temptation to look herself. That was the line she hadn't wanted to cross, because she understood what it would mean. Instruments could be calibrated. Animals could be studied. But if she looked through the aperture — if she put her eye to the lens and saw what the machine had opened — she would be crossing a threshold that couldn't be uncrossed. She would know. And knowing was not the same as suspecting.

···

She looked.

···

···

···

The first thing she saw was her laboratory. The same room, the same equipment, the same machine. But the woman standing at the console was not her.

···

The woman was shorter. Darker. She had the same posture, the same tilt of the head, but her features were different — sharper, more defined, the features of someone who had lived a different life and been shaped by different experiences. She was talking to someone off-frame, laughing at something, and her laugh was the same laugh that Yuki had heard in her own head a thousand times but had never heard from anyone else's throat.

···

Yuki stepped back. She breathed. She looked again.

···

The other laboratory was the same. The other woman was the same. She was not Yuki — she had a different face, a different history — but she was doing what Yuki was doing, in a room that looked exactly like this room, in a world that was exactly like this world.

···

She was looking back.

···

···

···

The discoveries accumulated over the following months. The machine could see into many worlds, not just one — an infinite stack of parallel realities, each one slightly different from the last, each one containing a version of Yuki who had made slightly different choices. In some worlds, she had not pursued physics; she was an architect, a musician, a mother of three. In some worlds, she had pursued physics but had not built the machine; she was dead, or working in a different field, or a bureaucrat at a research institute. In some worlds, she had built the machine but had never looked through it; she was still in the laboratory, still waiting, still safe in the ignorance of the untested hypothesis.

···

She mapped them, carefully, systematically, the way a cartographer maps the coastline of a new continent. She noted the differences. She tracked the patterns. She tried to understand which choices led to which outcomes, which branching points in her life had sent her down this path instead of that one.

···

She found the world where her father had not died.

···

In that world, she was not a physicist. She was a chef, or so the instruments suggested — she was standing in a kitchen, and the kitchen was full of the particular light that kitchens have when they are loved, and she was laughing at something a customer had said. Her father was there, older, white-haired, alive. He was setting tables. He was doing the work of a man who had not lost a son and had not spent the rest of his life in the grief that had defined his existence in Yuki's world.

···

Yuki looked at that world for a long time. She looked until she couldn't see anymore, and then she closed her eyes and saw it anyway, and she understood that the door between worlds was not just a window.

···

It was a wound.

···

···

···

The problem — the problem she hadn't anticipated, the problem she should have anticipated — was that she could not stop looking.

···

She should have been studying the physics. She should have been publishing papers, securing funding, building the academic career that the discovery warranted. But she was not studying the physics. She was studying the lives.

···

She found the world where she had married David Kowalski instead of divorcing him. In that world, they were still married, twenty years later, and they had two children who looked like combinations of both of them, and they were sitting on a porch in a house she didn't recognize, watching the sun go down. David was older. His hair was grey. He was holding her hand, and she was leaning against his shoulder, and they were not talking because they didn't need to talk. They had been married for twenty years, and they had learned the particular silence that comes when two people have said everything important and can now simply exist in each other's presence.

···

Yuki watched them until the sun went down in that world, and then she watched the darkness, and then she finally turned away from the aperture and sat in her chair and cried.

···

She found the world where the grant had been funded and she had built a different machine. In that world, the machine was larger, more sophisticated, connected to a network of other machines across a dozen institutions. The discovery was shared, debated, accepted. She was famous. She was respected. She was on magazine covers and talk shows and had done the thing she had always wanted to do: changed the world.

···

She watched herself give interviews. She watched herself smile at cameras. She watched the other Yuki be someone who had succeeded in the way that success is traditionally measured.

···

She watched until she couldn't stand it anymore, and then she stopped watching that world, and she did not go back.

···

···

···

The question she could not answer — the question that woke her at three in the morning and kept her awake until dawn — was which world was real.

···

She had always believed, without examining the belief, that her world was the real one. This was the default assumption, the baseline, the reference point against which all other possibilities were measured. Her world was the world where things had happened the way they had happened, and other worlds were variations, alternatives, imagined possibilities.

···

But she had looked through the door. She had seen the other sides. And in those other worlds, the people she saw — the other Yuki, the other David, the other father — they believed their worlds were real too. They had the same certainty she had. They had the same sense of being the center of things, the same conviction that they were the ones who had stayed behind in a universe of infinite possible selves.

···

If all of them were real, then none of them were. Or all of them were. That was the other possibility — the possibility that the word "real" didn't apply, that the question she was asking was malformed, that the framework she was using to think about this was too small for the thing she was looking at.

···

She didn't know. She couldn't know. That was the nature of the wound: it didn't heal, and it didn't close, and she was the one who had opened it and couldn't figure out how to stop bleeding.

···

···

···

The last time she looked through the aperture, she found a world she hadn't seen before. In that world, the laboratory was empty. The machine was there, but it was covered with a tarp, and there was dust on the floor, and the monitors were dark. The other Yuki — if there had been an other Yuki — was not there. Had not been there, probably, for a long time.

···

She looked at the empty laboratory for a long time, and she felt something she hadn't expected: peace.

···

This was a world where she had never built the machine. Or where she had built it and destroyed it. Or where something had happened — an accident, a failure, a choice — that had ended the research and left the laboratory dark.

···

In this world, there was no door between worlds. In this world, there was only one world, and the people in it lived their lives without knowing what they had given up, without knowing that other possibilities had existed and been left unexplored.

···

Yuki understood that this world was also a variation. She understood that it was one of infinitely many, layered like pages, separated by dimensions that the human mind was not equipped to perceive.

···

But it looked like peace. And after everything she had seen — after all the lives she had examined and all the choices she had traced — peace was not nothing.

···

She closed the aperture. She turned off the machine. She sat in the laboratory, in the quiet, in the particular silence of a world that contained only what it contained.

···

She was still here. She was still real. She didn't know if that was enough.

···

But it was what she had.
