You Tell the Story by Ellie Sunwood is built on a simple, elegant premise: show children a picture, then ask them what they think is happening. No correct answers. No pre-written story. Just the picture and the child's own imagination.

The draw-and-write format makes this a practical tool rather than a passive reading experience. Each spread presents an illustration — something a child can look at and interpret — and then leaves space for them to respond in words, in drawings, or both. This is not a book that requires the adult to read it aloud. A child can engage with it independently, which makes it especially useful for road trips, waiting rooms, or any situation where you want a screen-free alternative that actually holds attention.

The pedagogical case for open-ended creative prompts is well-supported by literacy research. When children narrate what they see in an image, they practice inference, vocabulary, sequencing, and expressive writing simultaneously — all without experiencing the process as homework.

What distinguishes this book from other children's activity books is its restraint. Sunwood resists the urge to add stickers, fold-outs, or other mechanical engagement tricks. The illustrations are clear and specific enough to spark stories without dictating them — a balance that is harder to achieve than it sounds. Every image in the book gives a child something concrete to respond to without telling them what to see, which is the core skill the book is designed to develop.

The book is also notable as a screen-free alternative that does not feel like a consolation prize for putting down a tablet. Children who are used to the immediate feedback loops of digital media can find traditional activity books tedious because they do not provide enough stimulation. You Tell the Story sidesteps this problem by making each page genuinely open-ended — there is no completion state, no right answer to find, which means a child can return to the same image multiple times and generate different stories each time.

For parents or educators looking for a tool that builds narrative thinking rather than just filling time, this book is worth having on hand. It works equally well as a quiet-time activity for a solo child or as a collaborative exercise between a parent and child taking turns with the same image. The format is simple enough to use immediately and interesting enough to return to.

## Why this book sits in our collection

You Tell the Story is included because it takes narrative craft seriously as a craft rather than as a mystical gift. The author is a working novelist and writing teacher, and the book reads like a workshop manual that respects the reader's intelligence. The book covers the basics of plot, character, dialogue, and prose rhythm, but it does not stop there — it covers the operational decisions that separate working writers from aspiring writers (how to handle a stalled draft, how to revise without losing the original energy, how to read published fiction as a craftsperson rather than as a fan).

## The argument that earns the reader's attention

The book's central claim is that narrative skill is learnable. Most craft books hedge on this; the author commits. The book shows working drafts alongside their revisions, names the specific decisions that drove each revision, and explains why a particular revision worked where an earlier attempt did not. The argument is not that anyone can write a great novel — the author does not claim that — but that anyone who is willing to do the work can write a good one.

## Where the book is strongest

The chapter on dialogue is the strongest. The author breaks down the function of dialogue (advancing plot, revealing character, creating rhythm) and shows how each function can be served without the others. The exercises at the end of the chapter are genuinely useful rather than busywork, and the worked examples include both well-known published work and the author's own drafts.

## Where the book is weaker

The chapter on genre conventions is shorter than it needs to be. A reader who has not yet read widely in their chosen genre will not get enough scaffolding from this chapter; a reader who has read widely will find it redundant. The author has written more thorough material elsewhere, and a pointer to that material would have served the book better.

## Who this book is for

Recommended for aspiring novelists in the early or middle stages of their craft development; for writing teachers building their own curriculum; for working writers looking for a refresher on the operational decisions they make every day but rarely articulate. Less useful for absolute beginners who do not yet know what genre they want to write in, and for experienced novelists looking for advanced material beyond the basics.
