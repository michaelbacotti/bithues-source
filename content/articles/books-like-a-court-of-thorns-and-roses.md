A Court of Thorns and Roses by Sarah J. Maas (2015) is the kind of book that arrives at exactly the right moment in a reader's life. The books in this guide share specific qualities with the original — character arc, setting, theme, or emotional register — so you can find your next read based on what made A Court of Thorns and Roses work for you.

## For the Same Beauty-and-the-Beast Arc

If it was the fairy-tale framing of Feyre's captivity that pulled you in:

### A Court of Mist and Fury by Sarah J. Maas (2016) — The sequel that transformed the arc

Fantasy, romantic fantasy, the Sarah J. Maas enterprise — a series that builds momentum with each installment. If the first book was a slow-burn enticement, the second is the detonation. A Court of Mist and Fury takes everything readers responded to in A Court of Thorns and Roses and refuses to play it safe again. The stakes climb, the world expands, and the protagonist's interiority shifts from reactive to actively chosen — a distinction that matters enormously. Maas has a gift for escalation without losing the emotional throughline that hooks readers in the first place. This is the book that convinced thousands of readers the series was worth following into deeper, darker territory. If you wanted more from Feyre by the time you closed Thorns, this is where you get it — and then some.

### House of Salt and Sorrows by Erin A. Craig (2018) — Twelve dancing sisters in a dark fairy-tale frame

House of Salt and Sorrows is a YA gothic fairy-tale retelling set in a coastal estate where twelve sisters are bound by a curse that claims them one by one. Like A Court of Thorns and Roses, Craig builds her world from the bones of familiar folklore, transforming a known story into something that feels both inevitable and surprising. The prose is atmospheric without tipping into excess — gothic dread builds through accumulated detail rather than spectacle. Craig's sisters are distinct, their grief rendered with care that grounds the supernatural in real emotional stakes. If you appreciated the way Maas recontextualized fairy-tale structures to explore power and desire, Craig offers a darker, more melancholy version of that same impulse — a story where the palace is haunted, the magic is costly, and the dancing never quite stops.

### The Bear and the Nightingale by Katherine Arden (2017) — Russian folklore with a brave heroine

The Bear and the Nightingale is a historical fantasy set in medieval Russia, steeped in the raw magic of Slavic folklore rather than the polished courts of fairy tale retellings. Where Maas gives us a heroine who descends into faerie and must survive by wit and will, Arden offers Vasilisa, a girl caught between her father's Christianity and the old pagan spirits still stirring in the woods. Both books explore what happens when a young woman discovers she possesses power the world around her insists she shouldn't have. Arden's prose is quieter, her stakes more communal — village survival against frost and famine rather than individual triumph. If what drew you to ACOTAR was that collision between the human and the otherworldly, and you want something that lingers in the bones rather than the blood, this is your next read.

## For the Fae Court Politics

If it was the scheming at the High Fae courts that made ACOTAR feel weighty:

### The Cruel Prince by Holly Black (2018) — Human protagonist in a faerie court political game

The Cruel Prince is YA dark fantasy set in Elfhame, Holly Black's luminous and lethal faerie world, where mortals are pawns and fae are players. Jude Duarte is ten years old when she watches her parents murdered by the fae king who then raises her as a political prop—a human reminder of fae superiority. She survives by learning the game: manipulation, betrayal, strategic cruelty. The book shares with A Court of Thorns and Roses a human protagonist navigating faerie court politics, but Black is colder, less forgiving, more interested in the cost of ambition than its rewards. This is not a romance that softens the world; it is a study of power and what it demands. If what pulled you through Feyre's story was the pressure of being outmatched in a beautiful, dangerous place, Elfhame will feel like coming home—except no one is safe, and no one gets to stay innocent.

### A Court of Wings and Ruin by Sarah J. Maas (2017) — War arc across High Fae courts

If A Court of Thorns and Roses pulled you into the politics and peril of Prythian's High Fae courts, the sequel delivers on every promise—and doubles the cost. A Court of Wings and Ruin is the war book the first volume was building toward: more battles, more betrayal, more impossible choices made under the weight of ancient alliances. What Maas does well here is raising the stakes not just in scale but in moral complexity—the good side is not always good, and victories carry bodies. The character work, particularly Feyre's, matures considerably. This is for readers who wanted the first book to go darker and wider. If you craved the political maneuvering and the cost of war, this is your next book.

[See Price on Amazon →](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=A+Court+of+Wings+and+Ruin+Sarah+J.+Maas&tag=bithues-20)

### The Wicked King by Holly Black (2019) — Sequel with deeper court intrigue

If A Court of Thorns and Roses pulled you into the machinery of fae politics, Holly Black's The Wicked King will show you what happens when the gears keep turning without mercy. Where Maas builds her world through romance and earned power, Black's sequel runs on something colder: ambition weaponized by a protagonist who is too clever by half and knows it. Jude has survived the Court of Owls, but survival is no longer enough—she wants the throne itself, and she'll maneuver anyone into its path. The fae here are not alluring in the way of Tamlin's Spring Court; they are alien, petty, and genuinely dangerous in ways that feel unsentimental and honest. If you want your fantasy sequels darker, sharper, and less concerned with being liked, this is the right next read.

## For the Maas Universe Specifically

If you want to read all of Maas's work in recommended order:

### Throne of Glass by Sarah J. Maas (2012) — Earlier Maas series, assassin protagonist

High fantasy meets coming-of-age in Maas's debut series, which follows Celaena Sardothien, a legendary assassin pulled from death row to compete in a king's brutal tournament. Where ACOTAR builds its world through a fairy-tale lens, Throne of Glass opens with its heroine already formidable — sharp, proud, and fully aware of her own lethal reputation. The romance unfolds slowly, layered across multiple books rather than arriving in the first act, which gives the slower early volumes room to build something more durable. The later books shift into something genuinely propulsive, with Celaena's dual identities and hidden histories creating real stakes. If you came to ACOTAR for a heroine who earns her power rather than receiving it, this series follows that instinct further, across a longer road.

[See Price on Amazon →](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Throne+of+Glass+Sarah+J.+Maas&tag=bithues-20)

### Crown of Midnight by Sarah J. Maas (2013) — Throne of Glass sequel

If A Court of Thorns and Roses by Sarah J. Maas pulled you in with its fairy-tale frame and its slow turn toward darkness, Crown of Midnight continues that trajectory in a different register. This is the second book in Maas's earlier Throne of Glass series, which follows Celaena Sardothien — assassin, champion, and eventually something far more dangerous to the crown that employs her. Where the ACOTAR sequel deepens Feyre's world through romance, Crown of Midnight deepens Celaena's through betrayal, discovery, and the realization that the people she trusted have been using her as a pawn. Maas writes the costs of cynicism well. If you came to ACOTAR for a heroine who survives by becoming indispensable, this is the same hunger in a different cage. The sequel hook is a body count that actually matters.

[See Price on Amazon →](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Crown+of+Midnight+Sarah+J.+Maas&tag=bithues-20)

### House of Earth and Blood by Sarah J. Maas (2020) — Crescent City, urban fantasy with Maas-style romance

Urban fantasy, contemporary setting. House of Earth and Blood shares with A Court of Thorns and Roses what might be called emotional architecture — a world built not just of lore but of the characters' hunger to belong within it. Where Prythian gave you a fae court as a stage for longing, Crescent City gives you a walled metropolis of angels, shifters, and fae as a stage for grief, ambition, and the slow work of trusting someone again. Maas writes romance the way architects design load-bearing walls: it is structural, not decorative. The plot is a procedural mystery wrapped around a larger supernatural conflict, and it moves with genuine velocity once it commits. If what kept you turning pages in Prythian was not the battles but the way characters circled each other before allowing themselves to be known, you will find that same patient, high-stakes intimacy here.

## Why A Court of Thorns and Roses Won't Be Replaced

A Court of Thorns and Roses by Sarah J. Maas succeeds because it understood something specific about its readers: that fantasy can be a place where intensity of feeling is part of the contract, where the stakes are not just the world but the heart. The books above take that promise seriously. None of them are exactly like A Court of Thorns and Roses, but each carries a piece of what made it work — and that is as close as the genre gets to a true successor.
