There is a quiet ambition that lives in most of us, even if we rarely speak it aloud: *I want to read more.* Not because reading is a chore to be checked off, but because we sense—correctly—that the person we will be in five years is partly determined by the books we let into our lives between now and then.

And yet, for all our good intentions, most of us read less than we want to. The culprit is rarely a lack of interest. It is usually a lack of system. This guide is not about discipline or willpower. It is about building an environment and a set of habits that make reading the natural, frictionless choice.

## Why"Read More" Is a Worthy Goal

Before diving into tactics, it is worth naming why this goal matters. Books are not the only valuable medium—essays, longform journalism, and podcasts all have their place—but books offer something distinct: sustained, developed thought. A novel or a carefully argued nonfiction work gives an author the space to take you somewhere, to change your mind over three hundred pages rather than in a two-minute segment. That depth is hard to replicate elsewhere.

Reading also shapes how we think and write. Vocabulary, sentence structure, the ability to hold a complex argument in mind—these are muscles that develop through practice. You do not get them from skimming headlines. You get them from following an author's thinking across a full arc.

There is also something more intangible. A well-read person has, in a sense, lived more lives, visited more eras, and sat with more minds than someone who consumes only the present moment. That breadth is its own reward.

## The Core Problem: Friction

The single biggest obstacle to reading more is not a shortage of time. It is friction. Every step between you and a book is a point where momentum dies. If the book you want to read is in another room, in a box, or in a format that requires effort to access, you will default to something easier—your phone, the television, a scroll through feeds.

The solution is the inverse: **reduce friction to near zero.**

This means having books physically present where you already spend time. It means having multiple reading options so you can always match your mood. It means having a reading device that goes everywhere your phone goes, so switching from reading to not-reading is never a logistical question.

## Practical Strategies That Actually Work

### Build a Large, Varied TBR Pile

"TBR" stands for "to be read," and serious readers treat it not as a source of guilt but as a well-stocked pantry. The goal of a large TBR pile is simple: always have your next book waiting.

A good TBR pile is varied. It should include short books and long ones, easy reads and demanding ones, fiction and nonfiction. Variety prevents the paralysis that comes from staring at a towering stack of "important" books you are not in the mood for. Some evenings you want to tear through a crime novel. Other evenings you want to wrestle with a philosophy text. Both count. Both matter.

Where do you find books? Used bookstores are an excellent source—they are cheap, they are curated by humans with taste, and they often contain gems you did not know you needed. Libraries remain one of the greatest resources available to any reader, particularly for new releases. Book clubs and recommendations from friends you trust are also reliable paths to your next great read.

### Set a Small, Non-Negotiable Minimum

One of the most effective tricks is to commit to reading as little as one page per day. This sounds absurdly small because it is. But the goal is not the page count—it is consistency. Once you sit down with the intention to read one page, you will almost always read more. The hard part is starting. A one-page commitment removes the psychological barrier to starting.

Over time, this compounds. Some weeks you will read twenty hours. Others, maybe two. But as long as you are returning to books regularly, you are building a habit, and habits are what survive busy seasons.

### Create a Reading Environment

Your environment shapes your behavior more than your intentions do. Take stock of where you read now versus where you could read. The goal is to make reading the easiest option in as many moments as possible.

This might mean keeping a book on your nightstand, another in your bag, and a third in the bathroom. It might mean a dedicated reading chair with good light and no television in sight. It might mean headphones and a "reading playlist"—instrumental music, brown noise, or ambient sound—that signals to your brain that it is time to focus.

Phone placement matters too. If your phone is next to you, it is not a reading environment. If your phone is in another room and your book is in your hands, the environment is working for you.

### Track Your Reading

There is something deeply satisfying about tracking what you read. It does not need to be elaborate—a simple list of titles and authors, perhaps with a few words about each. Goodreads, StoryGraph, or even a notebook will do.

The act of recording a finished book creates a small sense of accomplishment and provides a visible record of your reading life. Over months and years, this record becomes a testament to what you have done, which in turn motivates what you will do next.

### Read Multiple Books Simultaneously

The old advice to read one book at a time is, for most people, not good advice. Reading multiple books simultaneously is not only acceptable—it is often optimal. Different books serve different needs, and matching the right book to your current mood is part of the art of reading.

Try keeping three to five books in rotation at all times: a long serious work for quiet mornings, a novel for bedtime, a nonfiction book on a topic you are exploring, and perhaps a poetry collection or short story collection for waiting rooms or short breaks. This way, no matter how much time you have, there is always an appropriately sized book available.

## Book Recommendations to Get You Started

If you are looking for a reading plan but are not sure where to begin, here are some places to start. These are books that repay the time you invest in them and that tend to pull readers deeper into the habit.

## Fiction:

- *The Brothers Karamazov* by Fyodor Dostoevsky — A dense, profound novel about faith, family, and moral responsibility. It is long but nearly impossible to put down once you are inside it.
- *Middlemarch* by George Eliot — One of the greatest novels in the English language, a sweeping portrait of English provincial life that somehow makes every character feel like someone you know.
- *The Master and Margarita* by Mikhail Bulgakov — A wild, funny, terrifying novel about the Devil visiting Soviet Moscow. No other book quite like it.
- *Beloved* by Toni Morrison — A spare, devastating novel about slavery and its aftermath. Morrison's prose is unlike anything else in American literature.
- *The Remains of the Day* by Kazuo Ishiguro — A short, quiet novel about regret, duty, and the stories we tell ourselves about our own lives.

## Nonfiction:

- *Thinking, Fast and Slow* by Daniel Kahneman — A landmark book on decision-making and cognitive bias by a Nobel Prize-winning psychologist. Dense but rewarding.
- *The Power of Habit* by Charles Duhigg — A readable exploration of how habits work in individuals and organizations, with practical applications.
- *The Making of the Atomic Bomb* by Richard Rhodes — Exceptionally well-researched and compellingly written, this Pulitzer Prize winner tells the story of the making of the atomic bomb with the depth of a novel.
- *Salt: A World History* by Mark Kurlansky — A surprising and delightful book that traces the history of civilization through one of its most essential commodities. Proof that any subject can be fascinating in the right hands.
- *The Gene: An Intimate History* by Siddhartha Mukherjee — A sweeping history of genetics that is also a meditation on what it means to be human.

## For Building the Habit:

- *How to Read a Book* by Mortimer Adler and Charles Van Doren — An old classic that teaches you the art of reading actively and deeply. Especially valuable if you want to get more out of demanding books.
- *The Weight of Glory* by C.S. Lewis — A collection of superb essays, including "Learning in War-Time," on why literature matters and how it shapes the soul. Available in the collection of the same name or in "The Collected Shorter Papers."

## The Long View

Reading more is not a sprint. It is a practice, like exercise or meditation—the benefits compound over years, and the only real requirement is showing up consistently. You will not notice the change in a month. But compare yourself to where you were three years ago, and you will find a different person looking back at you: broader, deeper, more capable of nuance and more at ease with complexity.

Start small. Stack the deck in your favor. And then let the books do their work.

You already have the ambition. The system is the rest. Build it, tend it, and trust it. The books will be there.
