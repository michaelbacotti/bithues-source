There is a moment in Becky Chambers's *The Long Way to a Small, Angry Planet* when the crew of the spaceship *Wayfarer* — a crew made up of different species, different histories, different ways of being — sits down to a meal together. It is not a dramatic scene. Nothing explodes. No one delivers a monologue about the meaning of diversity. The crew members simply eat together, and the narration notes that this is not unusual, and that they do this all the time, and that for them it is unremarkable.

And then you, the reader, realize that this small, unremarkable act of people with nothing in common except their shared ship choosing to sit together and eat is, in the world as it actually exists, not unremarkable at all. It is radical. It is an act of faith in the possibility that different creatures can share a space without destroying each other.

That is hopepunk. It is not naive. It is not easy. It is the deliberate choice to imagine that things can get better, and to build the emotional and political vocabulary to pursue that imagining into reality.

## Where the Word Comes From

"Hopepunk" entered critical discourse around 2017-2018, primarily through the science fiction and fantasy communities online. The term was coined as a response to "grimdark" — the literary and genre movement, associated with writers like Joe Abercrombie and Mark Lawrence, that characterized fiction as brutal, morally ambiguous, and fundamentally skeptical that people are capable of good. Grimdark's thesis was essentially: the world is violent and people are selfish and anyone who believes otherwise is naive.

Hopepunk pushed back. The argument was not that grimdark was wrong about the world's darkness — it was that darkness is not a reason to stop imagining alternatives. The hopepunk position is: *yes, the world is full of injustice and violence and failure, and we can see that clearly, and also we can choose the future anyway.*

The companion term "solarpunk" emerged around the same time, with more explicit political and ecological dimensions — a movement in fiction and visual art that imagines futures rooted in community, sustainability, and mutual aid rather than corporate power and environmental collapse. Hopepunk and solarpunk overlap significantly. The difference is mostly emphasis: solarpunk is more concretely political, hopepunk is more emotionally focused.

## What Hopepunk Actually Feels Like

The hopepunk future is not a utopia. This is important. Hopepunk fiction tends to be set in worlds with real problems — systemic injustice, environmental damage, the legacies of violence and colonialism. The difference between hopepunk and naive optimism is that hopepunk knows the problems exist. It simply refuses to let the problems be the whole story.

The emotional signature of hopepunk is this: something difficult happens, and instead of the narrative suggesting that the difficulty proves nothing will ever change, it suggests that people are trying anyway, and that their trying matters, even if it doesn't fix everything. The emphasis is on the effort, the choice, the act of building something in a broken world rather than pretending the world is not broken.

This is a fundamentally different emotional register than the cynicism that dominates much of contemporary genre fiction. Hopepunk does not ask you to feel good about the world. It asks you to feel *capable* — to feel that the world is something you have a stake in, not something happening to you.

## The Books That Define It

### Becky Chambers

If you read one hopepunk author, read Becky Chambers. Her *Wayfarer* series — *The Long Way to a Small, Angry Planet*, *A Closed and Common Orbit*, *Record of a Spaceborn Few*, and *The Galaxy, and the Ground Within* — is the definitive hopepunk text. Each book follows different characters in the same universe, and each is about what it means to build connection across difference. Chambers writes about trauma and grief with extraordinary precision, and she also writes about community and chosen family with equal honesty. She does not flinch from pain, and she does not pretend pain is the final word.

*A Closed and Common Orbit* is perhaps the best entry point: an AI who has escaped a lifetime of servitude in a synthetic body must learn to exist as a person. The novel is about identity, embodiment, and what it means to be allowed to want things. It will make you cry and then make you feel capable.

### T. Kingfisher

Kingfisher writes in several genres, but her work consistently operates in hopepunk territory — not because her books are gentle, but because her characters choose kindness in situations where cruelty would be easier. *Paladin's Grace* (a romance between two middle-aged trauma survivors solving a murder) and *Minor Leagues of a Minor God* are excellent places to start.

Her standalone novel *What Moves the Dead* is a retelling of Edgar Allan Poe's "The Fall of the House of Usher" that is simultaneously a body horror novel and a meditation on community and friendship. The horror is real. The hope is also real. They are not in tension.

### N.K. Jemisin

Jemisin is not usually labeled as hopepunk, but the *Broken Earth* trilogy — beginning with *The Fifth Season* — is hopepunk at its most sophisticated. The world is catastrophic, the societies within it are unjust, and the characters carry generational trauma that has not been processed. And yet the trilogy is about the possibility of collective action, mutual aid, and the choice to build rather than destroy. Jemisin won three consecutive Hugo Awards for this series, a feat no one else has accomplished. The reason is not political fashion — it is that her work genuinely earns the emotional response it provokes.

### Octavia Butler

Butler is essential reading for anyone interested in hopepunk — or in any serious fiction about what it means to survive and build. *Parable of the Sower* and its sequel *Parable of the Talents* are set in a near-future America that has undergone climate collapse and societal fragmentation. They are not gentle books. Butler does not spare her readers from what that world looks like. But her protagonist, Lauren Olamina, chooses to build — a new community, a new set of beliefs, a new way of thinking about survival — and the books argue, with devastating clarity, that the alternative to building is not waiting. It is letting someone else decide what your future looks like.

*Kindred*, *Fledgling*, and *The Patternist* series extend Butler's exploration of what it means to choose each other across lines of power, race, and species. She is the grandmother of hopepunk, whether the term existed then or not.

### Ray Bradbury (An Honest Nod)

Bradbury's *The Martian Chronicles* is not hopepunk in the contemporary sense, but it is hopepunk in spirit — a collection of stories about humanity's attempts to reach Mars, most of which fail, some of which succeed, and all of which are suffused with a sense that the trying is what matters. Bradbury's Mars is a mirror for human longing and human failure, and the book's ending — a handful of humans who choose to stay, who choose the planet as home despite everything — is hopepunk before the word existed.

## Why It Matters

Hopepunk is not a trend. It is a response to a cultural moment.

The grimdark moment in genre fiction — roughly the 2000s through the early 2010s — was a legitimate artistic response to post-9/11 disillusionment, to the Iraq War, to the sense that liberal internationalism had failed and the future was not going to be what we were promised. Grimdark said: we see clearly now, and what we see is ugly. That was a real feeling, and it produced real art.

Hopepunk is the next feeling. Not a rejection of what we learned from grimdark, but a building upon it. We still see clearly. We still know the world is full of injustice. We are not naive. We are also not finished. The hopepunk response to "the world is broken" is: *yes, and we live here, so what are we building?*

This is not a small thing to imagine. Imagining a future worth living into is one of the hardest things a person can do — especially now, especially with everything that is happening politically and environmentally. Hopepunk fiction does that imaginative work for you. It shows you what building looks like. It shows you people choosing each other, choosing community, choosing to try. It is not escapism. It is rehearsal.

If you want to know what kind of person hopepunk makes you: it makes you braver. Because it shows you that courage is not the absence of fear. It is the choice to build something anyway.
