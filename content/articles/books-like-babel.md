Babel by RF Kuang (2022) is the kind of book that arrives at exactly the right moment in a reader's life. The books in this guide share specific qualities with the original — character arc, setting, theme, or emotional register — so you can find your next read based on what made Babel work for you.

## For the Anti-Colonial Core

If it was Kuang's interrogation of empire and translation that made Babel feel urgent:

### The Poppy War by RF Kuang (2018) — Kuang's earlier trilogy, also Chinese history-inspired

The Poppy War (2018) is the first book in RF Kuang's grim Fantasy Fiction trilogy, drawing on the Sino-Japanese War and centuries of Chinese history with the same unflinching rigor she would later bring to Babel. Where Babel examines the violence embedded in language and commerce, The Poppy War traces what happens when a gifted orphan from a marginalized province masters an ancient shamanic tradition and is thrust into an escalating war that consumes everything she loves. The quality they share is not mere setting but structural honesty: both novels refuse to let their protagonists—or their readers—off easy once the machinery of empire begins to turn. Kuang's prose here is leaner, her pace more punishing; this is not the measured academic architecture of Babel but something that accelerates into atrocity and moral compromise. Worth reading if you want to follow Kuang into darker territory and see her apply her clinical historical imagination to warfare, trauma, and what power costs.

### Yellowface by RF Kuang (2023) — Contemporary literary fiction, also by Kuang

Yellowface is contemporary literary fiction—a novel about the American publishing industry, about who gets published and why, and about the complicated architecture of literary credibility. Where Babel used the historical framework of Oxford's translation bureau to examine the violence embedded in language and empire, Yellowface turns that same analytical pressure onto the present-day book world. Kuang writes two writers: one white, one Chinese American, both ambitious, both grasping for the same prizes. The novel works not through melodrama but through the quiet accumulation of uncomfortable truths about gatekeeping, authenticity, and what it means to tell stories you didn't live. If Babel left you thinking about who controls the narrative, Yellowface will leave you thinking about who controls the publication—and how hard it is to see your own complicity.

### The Fifth Season by NK Jemisin (2015) — Apocalyptic epic with oppressive-systems critique

If Babel left you thinking about how language bends power—and how power bends people back—NK Jemisin's The Fifth Season will take that thread and set it on fire. This is secondary-world fantasy that reads like climate fiction, set on a continent fractured by seismic violence and social stratification so complete it feels inevitable. Jemisin writes from multiple perspectives across centuries, and the structural choices she makes aren't tricks—they're the argument. The Stillness isn't just dying; it's being managed, weaponized, silenced. What makes it essential is that Jemisin never lets the world's collapse eclipse the people surviving inside it. If you want a book that earns its devastation and doesn't flinch from what it costs, this is it.

## For the Dark Academia Setting

If it was the Oxford setting and the cutthroat intellectual competition that made Babel work:

### The Secret History by Donna Tartt (1992) — The modern dark academia classic

The Secret History is Donna Tartt's 1992 dark academia debut — a contemporary of Babel, though Tartt's novel operates in the morally bankrupt corridors of a Vermont liberal arts college rather than Kuang's Oxford. What binds both books is the corrupting power of beauty and intellect: characters who believe aesthetic perfection excuses moral catastrophe, culminating in an act of violence that reads as Greek tragedy played out in lecture halls and snow. Tartt's prose is deliberately lush, her narrator unreliable and complicit in ways that implicate the reader alongside the perpetrators. If you found Babel's meditation on language's complicity in empire unsettling in the best way, Tartt's examination of how beauty becomes a tool of evasion and justification will unsettle you differently — and stay with you longer.

[See Price on Amazon →](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=The+Secret+History+Donna+Tartt&tag=bithues-20)

### If We Were Villains by ML Rio (2017) — Shakespearean drama at a conservatory

Literary fiction, dark academia, contemporary. ML Rio's debut unfolds at an elite classical conservatory where ten students spend four years immersed in Shakespeare, Greek tragedy, and the dangerous intimacies of collaborative performance. What it shares with Babel is the way an institution shapes its inhabitants into instruments of its own logic—here, the plays become a lens through which friendship curdles into something far darker. Rio writes with precise, atmospheric control, building dread through accumulated gesture rather than spectacle. If you appreciated how Babel examined what it costs to hold knowledge in an unjust system, you'll find similar questions here: what happens when the art you're devoted to demands everything, including the truth?

[See Price on Amazon →](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=If+We+Were+Villains+ML+Rio&tag=bithues-20)

### The Atlas Six by Olivie Blake (2021) — Six magicians competing for a single prize

Dark academia lives in the tension between knowledge and corruption, and few books weaponize that tension as precisely as Olivie Blake's The Atlas Six. Where Babel explored how translation reshapes power, Blake's novel asks what happens when six brilliant practitioners of hermetic arts are sequestered in a London library with a single available position among them — cooperation becomes strategic suicide. The writing is elegant and cold, full of characters who perform friendship while calculating betrayal, and the novel earns its comparisons to a more literary Hunger Games by refusing to soften the cruelty at its center. The plot is slow-building and sometimes frustratingly interior, prioritizing psychological chess over spectacle. If you finished Babel wanting more books that treat intelligence itself as a dangerous, corrupting force — not a superpower but a burden — this is your next read.

## For the Magical Language System

If it was Kuang's core conceit — that magic works through translation loss — that hooked you:

### The Name of the Wind by Patrick Rothfuss (2007) — University magic with obsessive name-lore

If Babel's meticulous excavation of language's power to shape empire left you wanting more worlds built on the weight of words, Patrick Rothfuss's The Name of the Wind is the logical next step. Where Kuang dissects colonialism through translation, Rothfuss builds a universe where knowing a thing's true name grants literal control over it — a premise he executes with disciplined restraint rather than spectacle. Kvothe is an unreliable narrator, yes, but the self-mythology is part of the point: stories are how we make sense of experience, even when they mislead us. The prose is precise and addictive, the university setting grounded in real intellectual community. Worth reading if you want a book that takes its magic system seriously and follows through on the idea that words are not just tools but weapons and keys.

[See Price on Amazon →](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=The+Name+of+the+Wind+Patrick+Rothfuss&tag=bithues-20)

### A Deadly Education by Naomi Novik (2020) — School for magicians with monster-rich internal world

Babel by RF Kuang examines how knowledge and translation participate in empire. Naomi Novik's A Deadly Education asks a related question at a more elemental level: what happens when the school itself is the monster? The Scholomance is a London academy for young magicians where the curriculum is lethal and the graduation requirements include surviving four years without being eaten by the creatures that roam its halls. Novik shares with Kuang a willingness to let institutions be genuinely monstrous, not merely indifferent. The magic system here is visceral, tied to survival and accumulation rather than linguistics. If you wanted Babel to go darker, more primal — if the academic framing felt too civilized for what you were actually hungry for — Novik strips away the Oxford polish and shows you the teeth underneath.

[See Price on Amazon →](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=A+Deadly+Education+Naomi+Novik&tag=bithues-20)

### Piranesi by Susanna Clarke (2020) — Quiet, mysterious, house-of-magic with hidden lore

Piranesi is a literary fantasy set in a version of London that exists inside a vast, labyrinthine house whose rooms stretch endlessly in all directions, filled with statues and tidal water. Where Babel explored the violence embedded in language and translation, Piranesi asks what happens to identity when the world itself becomes unknowable — its narrator piecing together a reality from fragments, just as readers of Kuang's novel had to navigate competing truths. Clarke builds dread from accumulation rather than action, and the mystery unfolds with quiet, terrible logic. If what you craved in Babel was the sensation of being inside a system too large to fully understand, this is the book that continues that feeling.

## Why Babel Won't Be Replaced

Babel by RF Kuang succeeds because it understood something specific about its readers: that fantasy can be a place where intensity of feeling is part of the contract, where the stakes are not just the world but the heart. The books above take that promise seriously. None of them are exactly like Babel, but each carries a piece of what made it work — and that is as close as the genre gets to a true successor.
